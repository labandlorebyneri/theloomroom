Why being on every channel is not a channel strategy

How the Paradox of Choice changed the way I think about distribution, attention, and deciding which platforms actually deserve investment.

By Neri Published 19 September 2026 Applied channel strategy + behavioral science
Tags Channel Strategy · Social Media Marketing · SEO / AEO / GEO Marketing & Strategy
Framework The Channel Choice Filter

A brand can be everywhere and still have no channel strategy.

Every new platform can look like another opportunity: another audience, another place to publish, another chance to be discovered.

But every channel also creates another set of decisions.

What belongs there? Who owns it? What does the audience expect? How frequently should it be maintained? How should success be measured? And what receives less attention because this channel now exists?

The short answer

A channel strategy is not a list of places where a brand has an account. It is a set of decisions about which channels deserve attention, what job each one performs, how they work together, and what the organization is deliberately choosing not to prioritize. Presence is not strategy. A channel earns its place when it has a defined job in the customer journey.

01

Context

The number of available channels keeps growing. Human attention does not.

Marketing teams have more distribution choices than ever. A brand can maintain social profiles, video channels, messaging apps, search visibility, forums, newsletters, communities, directories, review profiles, partnerships, events, and owned media.

That abundance can be useful. It can also create a prioritization problem.

In my current role, I am working through exactly that problem for a multi-location service business. You can see more of the scope I manage on my Experience page.

The organization already maintains a broad platform mix across major social networks, video platforms, discussion forums, professional networks, and visual discovery platforms. We are also evaluating whether another messaging channel deserves a place in the system.

Our problem is not a lack of places to distribute content.

The harder question is:

What is each channel actually supposed to do?

That is the same diagnostic instinct behind Why the problem matters more than the first idea: before adding another tactic, define the problem the tactic is supposed to solve.

02

Problem

Every available channel can start to look like a channel we should use.

This reminds me of the broader idea popularized as the Paradox of Choice.

Iyengar and Lepper's well-known experiments found circumstances in which larger choice sets attracted attention but smaller choice sets were associated with greater subsequent purchasing or task engagement.1

Later meta-analytic work found that choice-overload effects are not universal and depend substantially on context and moderating conditions.2

That distinction matters.

I am not claiming that more marketing channels automatically cause worse marketing.

I use the Paradox of Choice as a strategic lens for a different problem:

As the number of available channels expands, the organization needs a stronger system for deciding which options deserve scarce attention.

Availability can masquerade as necessity. A platform exists, so perhaps we should be there. A competitor appears there, so perhaps we need an account too.

The result can become a collection of channels accumulated over time rather than a deliberately designed distribution system.

03

What I observed

Channel proliferation creates work long after the account is created.

Opening an account is usually the easy part.

Maintaining one is where the strategic cost appears.

Every active channel creates questions around audience, creative format, publishing cadence, moderation, replies, adaptation, reporting, optimization, attribution, governance, and ownership.

The real cost of adding a channel is the attention required to make that channel strategically useful.

This is also why more content is not always the answer. If the real constraint is capacity, distribution, or differentiation, adding another publishing obligation can make the system noisier rather than stronger.

If the same piece of content appears everywhere with minimal adaptation, I have to ask whether each platform is performing a different role.

If every channel requires original production, I have to ask whether the organization has enough creative capacity to support all of them properly.

If several channels reach similar audiences at similar stages of the journey, I have to ask whether the overlap creates reinforcement or simply duplication.

The same problem can also happen inside a platform. Multiple accounts may make complete strategic sense if they serve different audiences, propositions, geographies, or content functions. But if their roles cannot be clearly distinguished, channel proliferation can happen within a platform, not just between platforms.

04

What I did

I started treating channel selection as an exercise in prioritization, not expansion.

I use a framework I call The Channel Choice Filter.

The Channel Choice Filter
Audience→ Intent→ Role→ Distinct Value→ Capacity→ Trade-off→ Measurement

Audience

Are the people we actually need to reach meaningfully present here?

A platform can have an enormous user base and still be a poor strategic fit. The question is not whether the channel is popular. It is whether the channel contains an audience relevant to the problem we are trying to solve.

Intent

What are people doing when they use this channel?

The same person can search on one platform, scroll for entertainment on another, ask questions elsewhere, watch longer educational material on another, and use messaging when they are closer to direct interaction.

Role

What job does this channel perform in the journey?

Discovery, education, consideration, community, reputation, conversion, retention, customer interaction, or reinforcement are different jobs. If every channel is described as being for “awareness and engagement,” the roles probably have not been defined precisely enough.

Distinct value

What does this channel contribute that the existing system does not?

A new platform should not automatically be added because it creates another possible touchpoint. It should add something: a different audience, behavior, format, communication path, discovery mechanism, or function the current system cannot perform well.

When evaluating whether a messaging platform should be added to an existing mix, I would not ask only whether we should be there. I would ask:

What problem would this channel solve that the current system does not solve well enough?

If the answer is unclear, not yet is a valid channel strategy.

And if the channel sits closer to direct inquiry or follow-up, it should connect coherently to the rest of the conversion system. That is the same post-lead problem explored in Why the lead is not the conversion.

Capacity

Can we operate this channel well enough for it to deserve a place in the strategy?

Having an account is not the same as having the capacity to manage it. A functioning channel may require content, creative production, adaptation, moderation, customer responses, analytics, testing, and ongoing optimization.

Trade-off

What gets less attention if we choose this channel?

Creative capacity is finite. Analytical capacity is finite. Budget is finite. Attention is finite.

Adding a platform therefore has an opportunity cost.

The question is not merely: Can we do this? It is: What will we stop, reduce, delay, or deprioritize if this becomes important?

Measurement

What would success mean specifically for this channel?

Not every platform should be judged by immediate conversion. One may create discovery, another direct inquiries, another reputation, another retention, and another answer-seeking behavior.

If every channel is evaluated against the same metric, useful channels can be removed and weak ones preserved for the wrong reasons.

05

Why I chose that approach

Strategy requires exclusion.

The Paradox of Choice gives me a useful way to think about this.

The more options available, the easier it becomes to postpone the harder act of prioritization.

Keeping every channel feels safe because nothing has been excluded.

But refusing to choose is itself a choice. Every account still consumes some amount of organizational attention.

A channel strategy is partly an exercise in deliberate exclusion.

The objective is not to reduce channels for the sake of having fewer channels. The objective is to reduce ambiguity.

A broad omnichannel strategy can involve many channels when the roles are clear and the channels reinforce one another.

But omnichannel does not mean every channel.

Being everywhere without knowing why is a distribution footprint.

Being everywhere is a distribution footprint. Knowing why you are in each place is a channel strategy.

06

What happened

The audit is still in progress.

This is not a retrospective success story where I already know which channels will stay and which will go.

I am still working through the system.

The purpose is not necessarily to eliminate platforms. It is to determine which channels deserve continued investment, which need clearer roles, which may need stronger content differentiation, which overlap productively, which duplicate one another, which properties may need consolidation, and whether any new channel solves a sufficiently important problem to justify adding it.

That includes examining situations where multiple accounts exist inside one platform.

Their existence is not automatically inefficient. But they should be able to explain themselves strategically.

Do they serve different audiences? Different subject areas? Different jobs? Does the separation improve the system, or merely split production resources?

Those are more useful questions than follower count alone.

07

What this taught me

More distribution can create less strategic attention.

More channels can create more discovery opportunities, more audience touchpoints, and greater resilience when one platform changes.

But every additional active channel also increases the number of things the marketing system has to notice, produce, coordinate, measure, and improve.

The more places the brand appears, the easier it can become to spread the attention required to make any one of those places genuinely useful.

This is why I do not think the objective should be channel minimization.

The objective is channel intentionality.

A large channel portfolio with clearly differentiated roles may be strategically strong. A small channel portfolio with no coherent logic may still be weak.

The number is not the strategy.

The architecture is.

Important exception

Directories are not the same as active channels.

I would not apply the same reduction logic mechanically to reputable business directories, local listings, review platforms, and other authoritative profiles.

They solve a different problem.

A social or content channel usually creates an ongoing publishing obligation. It needs content, moderation, responses, creative adaptation, and measurement.

A reputable directory listing generally performs a different function.

For a service business—particularly a multi-location one—it can help customers and search systems establish basic facts about the organization.

Google explicitly recommends keeping Business Profile information complete and accurate and states that complete and accurate information can improve a business's ability to appear in local results.3

Google's Organization structured-data documentation also supports the use of sameAs URLs to point to external organization profiles and other pages that provide additional information about the entity.4

This distinction becomes even more relevant as search expands beyond a traditional ranked results page. As explored in Why ranking is no longer the whole search strategy, modern discoverability increasingly involves retrieval, corroboration, answerability, and visibility across multiple search and generative surfaces.

Bing has also emphasized accurate local-business information in the context of AI-generated location-based experiences and recommends maintaining current business information through Bing Places.5

I therefore separate:

active channel proliferation

from

entity and discovery coverage.

For an active channel, my question is:

Does this deserve the ongoing attention required to operate it?

For a reputable directory or listing, the question becomes:

Is this an authoritative place where customers or discovery systems may reasonably expect to verify who we are?

That does not mean every directory on the internet deserves a listing. Nor would I claim that every directory citation is a direct SEO, AEO, or GEO ranking factor.

The more defensible principle is that consistent, accurate information across relevant authoritative sources can reduce ambiguity around the business entity and support discoverability.

Channel strategy sometimes requires subtraction. Discoverability strategy sometimes requires corroboration.

08

How I would apply it elsewhere

Give every active channel a job description.

If I inherited a large channel portfolio tomorrow, I would not begin by closing accounts.

I would map them.

Audience: Who are we actually trying to reach here?

Intent: What are they doing when they encounter us?

Role: What part of the journey does this channel support?

Distinct value: What can this platform do that our other channels cannot do as well?

Content requirement: What does the platform need from us to function properly?

Ownership: Who is responsible for maintaining it?

Capacity: Can we realistically sustain that responsibility?

Trade-off: What resources does this channel consume?

Measurement: What evidence would justify continued investment?

Then I would classify each active channel:

Invest

It has a clear strategic job and deserves more support.

Maintain

It performs a useful role without requiring disproportionate investment.

Redefine

The platform may be valuable, but its purpose or content model is unclear.

Consolidate

Two or more properties may be solving the same problem unnecessarily.

Experiment

The hypothesis is promising but still needs evidence.

Deprioritize

The channel currently consumes more attention than its strategic contribution justifies.

Verify and maintain

For reputable directories and entity profiles where accuracy and consistency are the primary strategic purpose.

That last category prevents the audit from becoming a simplistic exercise in deleting platforms.

The broader lesson

The goal is not fewer channels. The goal is fewer unexplained channels.

The difficult part of channel strategy is no longer finding somewhere to publish.

There are plenty of places.

The difficult part is deciding what deserves sustained attention.

In my current situation, the answer may ultimately involve many of the channels we already have.

Some may need clearer jobs. Some may require stronger differentiation. Some may deserve more investment. Others may eventually become lower priorities.

And a new channel may still be added if it solves a problem the current system cannot solve effectively.

The goal is not fewer channels.

The goal is fewer unexplained channels.

Which channels deserve a role in the system—and what evidence earns them that role?

Presence is not strategy. Strategy begins when every channel has a reason to exist.

Scope

Where this framework applies—and where more testing is needed.

This Insight draws on an active channel-strategy problem within a multi-location service business with a relatively broad digital footprint.

The observations should not be interpreted as evidence that a particular number of channels is optimal or that reducing channels necessarily improves marketing performance.

Channel requirements vary substantially by audience, organization size, category, geography, resources, customer journey, regulatory environment, and business model.

The directory exception is also context-dependent. Reputable, relevant listings can support customer verification and local or entity discovery, but indiscriminate submission to low-quality directories should not be confused with a deliberate SEO, AEO, or GEO strategy.

Further measurement is required before determining which specific channels in the current portfolio should receive greater investment, consolidation, experimentation, or deprioritization.

Experience note. The organizational example in this Insight has been deliberately anonymized. Identifying business details, account names, locations, campaign information, and performance data have been omitted while preserving the strategic problem being evaluated.
Research use note. The choice-overload research referenced here examines human decision-making rather than marketing-channel portfolio design. The Channel Choice Filter, its application to organizational channel selection, and the distinction between active channel proliferation and entity/discovery coverage are my own strategic synthesis. Choice-overload effects are not universal. I therefore use the Paradox of Choice as a lens for thinking about marketing prioritization, not as evidence that maintaining more channels inherently reduces performance.
