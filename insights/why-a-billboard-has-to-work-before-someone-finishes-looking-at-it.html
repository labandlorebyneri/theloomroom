OOH · Attention · Applied Strategy

Why a billboard has to work before someone finishes looking at it

How campaign objectives, limited attention, and viewing context should shape out-of-home creative.

By Neri Published 20 September 2026 Applied OOH strategy + behavioral science
Tags Out-of-Home (OOH) Advertising · Brand Marketing · Asset Development
Framework The Billboard Glance Test

A billboard can be physically enormous and still have very little time to communicate.

That contradiction is what makes out-of-home advertising different from many other marketing assets.

A landing page can wait for someone to scroll. An email can sit in an inbox. A social post can be paused or revisited.

A billboard may be passed at speed.

The available surface is large.

The available attention may be tiny.

The short answer

OOH effectiveness starts with the objective. If the goal is awareness, the creative may need to do little more than get noticed, make the advertiser recognizable, and leave one thing behind in memory. If the goal is conversion, recognition is not enough. The audience also needs enough comprehension to understand why the message matters and what to do next. The right billboard is the one with the least unnecessary information for the job it has been given.

01

Context

I was designing for commuters, not for a design review.

In my current role, one of the changes I made involved the billboard system for a multi-location service business. Some placements sit around bridges and are encountered primarily by driving commuters.

That viewing condition mattered.

People were not standing in front of the creative examining every decision. They were passing it.

Earlier executions had used yellow and later blue as dominant billboard colors. Last year, we moved in a much more reduced direction.

The board became a strong red field. The only text was the brand name.

No explanatory headline. No service list. No lengthy call to action.

One dominant visual field. One name.

The decision was not based on a belief that red universally performs better than yellow or blue. It came from a more fundamental question:

What should someone be able to take away if they only give us a glance?

That is the same diagnostic principle behind Why the problem matters more than the first idea. Before deciding what the creative should contain, I needed to define the communication problem.

But OOH introduced another variable: the objective.

For this execution, the reduced approach made the most sense because the work leaned heavily toward awareness and recognition. Had the objective been direct conversion, I would have evaluated the layout differently.

02

Problem

A large format does not create unlimited attention.

Roadside advertising competes with something far more important than advertising: driving.

Costa and colleagues studied drivers along a 30-kilometer route containing 154 roadside advertising signs. Across the study, only about 24% of those signs received a fixation, and the median fixation lasted about 297 milliseconds. Gaze behavior also varied with factors including sign category, character count, placement, and driving speed.1

A separate driving-simulator experiment by Meuleners and colleagues examined electronic billboards and found that billboard characteristics affected driving performance and visual attention. Among the conditions tested, simpler billboard content and longer dwell times produced the least negative effects.2

Neither study tells marketers that there is one universally effective billboard layout.

They support a more useful premise:

Roadside creative operates inside an attentional constraint.

For driver-facing OOH, the goal should not be to hold attention for as long as possible. It should be to communicate without requiring prolonged attention away from the primary task.

That changes the creative question from How much can we put here? to How little does the audience need in order for this objective to work?

03

What I observed

A billboard can be perfectly legible and still communicate badly.

Legibility and comprehension are not the same thing.

A billboard may technically contain a readable headline, supporting copy, phone number, URL, CTA, image, logo, service list, and disclaimer.

But the audience still has to decide where to look, decode those elements, determine which ones matter, understand the proposition, and retain something useful before the exposure ends.

That is also why the principle in When more content is not the answer applies to creative assets. Every additional element becomes another candidate for attention.

Processing-fluency research provides one useful psychological lens. Reber, Schwarz, and Winkielman describe how properties such as figure-ground contrast, repetition, symmetry, and familiarity can affect the ease with which a stimulus is processed.3

I would not turn that into a rule that says simpler creative is automatically better. Complexity can create intrigue. Unexpected creative can interrupt habitual attention. A difficult idea can sometimes reward the effort required to understand it.

The important distinction is:

Is the difficulty serving the idea, or is it merely making the idea harder to retrieve?

For the commuter billboard I worked on, I wanted almost no internal competition. The visual sequence became essentially:

brand field → brand name

The hierarchy was immediate because there was very little else to negotiate.

04

The objective determines what effectiveness means

“Keep it simple” is useful advice only after the job has been defined.

A brand-awareness board and a direct-response board do not have identical cognitive jobs.

Objective What the viewer primarily needs to do Creative priority
AwarenessNotice and rememberDistinctive, low-friction brand cue
RecognitionIdentify the advertiserConsistent name and recognizable assets
ComprehensionUnderstand the propositionClear hierarchy and sufficient explanation
ConsiderationDecide whether it is relevantValue, differentiation, credibility
ConversionTake a next stepComprehension plus a realistic, low-friction response

For awareness, the communication path can be remarkably short:

Notice → Recognize → Remember

That can make a dominant visual field and a single brand identifier strategically powerful. The audience does not need to learn everything about the business during the exposure. The board may simply need to increase the chance that the name is familiar later.

Conversion creates a different requirement.

Now the audience may need to answer:

What is being offered? Is this relevant to me? Why should I care? What should I do?

That requires more comprehension.

But adding more information is not the answer by itself. The challenge is finding the minimum amount of information required to justify the response.

The more action you ask from the audience, the more comprehension the creative has to earn.

That is why I would not argue that the stripped-down awareness billboard should become the default template for every future OOH execution. If the objective changes, the layout may need to change with it.

05

What I did

I started evaluating outdoor creative with the Billboard Glance Test.

The Billboard Glance Test
Objective→ Context→ Notice→ Recognize→ Understand→ Remember→ Respond

Objective

What must happen because this billboard exists?

Awareness, recognition, comprehension, consideration, and direct response are related, but they are not interchangeable. Without that decision, the creative team cannot know which information is essential.

Context

Under what conditions will the advertisement actually be encountered?

Someone driving at speed does not have the same attentional conditions as someone waiting at a train station. A pedestrian does not experience an asset in the same way as a passenger. A repeated commuter exposure is different from a one-time encounter.

A billboard is not designed for a surface. It is designed for a person encountering that surface under a particular attentional condition.

Notice

What wins the first competition for attention?

Contrast, scale, typography, imagery, placement, and the surrounding environment all affect this. But getting noticed is not enough. The strategically important element also needs to benefit from that attention.

Recognize

Can the audience identify whose message this is?

For awareness-led OOH, that can be the principal task. The brand should not require several seconds of decoding before it becomes identifiable.

Understand

How much does someone need to understand for the objective to succeed?

An awareness campaign may require very little explanation. A conversion campaign usually needs more. A board can be beautifully minimal and strategically incomplete if someone recognizes the advertiser but has no idea why the message matters.

Remember

What should remain after the physical exposure is gone?

It is rarely realistic to expect someone to retain everything on a dense board. The creative should establish priority. For the awareness execution I worked on, the desired verbal takeaway was primarily the brand name.

This is the point where Part I deliberately stops and Part II begins: once attention has been won, the next problem is whether recognizable brand cues survive long enough to support memory.

Respond

What response is realistic in this context?

Someone driving should not be expected to interact with a complicated CTA. A stationary pedestrian may be able to scan a QR code. A commuter might instead remember the advertiser and search later. The correct response mechanism depends on both objective and environment.

06

Viewing context changes what the billboard can ask for

Driving is the clearest example because attention belongs primarily to the road. That supports highly compressed communication.

Walking is different. Pedestrians may have more visual freedom, but they are still navigating traffic, storefronts, phones, other people, and their destination. That may allow a street-level asset to support a secondary information layer that would be inappropriate for a high-speed roadside execution.

Stationary environments change the equation again. Bus shelters, stations, queues, and similar placements can support longer dwell time and therefore potentially more detailed communication.

Social context matters too.

Gallup and colleagues tracked 3,325 pedestrians in natural crowds and found that visual attention could spread socially: passersby became more likely to adopt a gaze direction as a stimulus group increased, before the response saturated.4

That study concerns socially transmitted attention, not advertising effectiveness. I would not use it to claim that groups make billboards perform better.

The useful lesson is narrower:

What receives attention in a public environment is influenced not only by the asset but also by what people around us are doing.

Driver-facing creative should survive a very brief glance. Walking environments may support a secondary information layer. High-dwell placements may support deeper explanation or interaction. Repeated commuter locations can rely on accumulation across exposures.

The medium may still be called OOH in every case. The cognitive conditions are not the same.

07

What happened

The creative direction is promising, but the attribution is not yet strong enough for a causal claim.

The attribution available for our branded social work has historically been more robust than what we had around the billboard program.

Digital environments can provide relatively direct signals around engagement, traffic, campaign behavior, and conversion paths. OOH is more difficult.

Someone may pass the billboard repeatedly, encounter a social asset later, perform a branded search, speak to someone about the business, and eventually convert through another touchpoint.

That makes it difficult to isolate the contribution of one billboard treatment.

So I cannot responsibly say that the red board and reduced copy caused a specific business outcome.

What I can say is that the direction has been promising enough to inform how we think about the next generation of billboard layouts. And as our attribution improves, we can make those decisions with greater confidence.

Not certainty.

Greater confidence.

Better attribution does not make OOH perfectly measurable. It makes the next creative decision less dependent on assumption.

It also gives us an opportunity to evaluate different objectives more appropriately. An awareness-focused board should not be judged only by last-touch conversion. A conversion-focused board should not be declared successful simply because people remember seeing it.

The measurement has to follow the objective.

08

What this taught me

Simplicity is not the strategy. Alignment is.

The most important thing I took from this work was not that billboards should contain one word.

It was:

The creative burden should match the communication objective and the amount of attention the environment makes available.

For awareness, reduction can be powerful because every unnecessary element competes with recognition.

For conversion, reduction has a limit. At some point the audience needs enough information to understand what the advertiser wants them to do and why doing it makes sense.

That is also why the lesson connects to Why being on every channel is not a channel strategy. Creative should reflect the behavior of the medium.

A social asset can carry a different information load from a bridge billboard. A station poster can behave differently from a highway board. A brand does not become inconsistent because the layouts differ.

The execution is doing its job when the recognizable system survives while the amount of information adapts to the channel.

The broader lesson

The best billboard leaves the right thing behind.

There is no universally effective billboard layout.

The correct layout depends on what the campaign needs to accomplish and what the audience can realistically process while encountering it.

For awareness:

Notice me. Recognize me. Remember me.

For conversion:

Recognize me. Understand why this matters. Know what to do next.

That difference should shape the hierarchy, information load, CTA, and measurement plan.

A billboard has only moments to work. The strategy determines what those moments need to accomplish.

The best billboard is not necessarily the one that says the least or the one that says the most. It is the one that leaves the right thing behind for the objective it was given.

Part II tomorrow

But that creates another problem.

Reducing a billboard to one dominant color and one brand cue can make sense when the objective is awareness.

But awareness is only useful if something survives the exposure.

That raised a different question for me:

If the billboard disappears from view within seconds, what makes the brand recognizable the next time someone sees it?

That becomes especially important when the same person passes the same location repeatedly. At that point, OOH is no longer only a problem of attention.

It becomes a problem of memory.

The color. The name. The typography. The visual treatment. The repetition.

Each exposure can either reinforce the same mental pattern or ask the audience to learn something new.

And I have seen what happens when those patterns are changed.

In another project, I worked with a public-facing professional whose established visual identity was replaced as the brand entered a new stage. The transition had strategic reasons, but it also disrupted something we had already spent time building: familiarity.

Because once attention has been won, the next problem is not Did they see us?

It is:

Will they know it is us the next time?

Scope

Where this framework applies—and where more evidence is needed.

The applied example in this Insight comes from OOH work for a multi-location service business. The organization, locations, brand name, campaign details, and identifying creative information have been generalized.

The example should not be interpreted as evidence that red, one-word copy, or extreme creative reduction universally improves billboard performance.

Its usefulness is narrower: it demonstrates how an awareness-led objective, limited commuter attention, and a desire for stronger recognition influenced a specific creative decision.

OOH effectiveness varies with placement, audience, speed, dwell time, media weight, category, brand familiarity, creative quality, repetition, campaign integration, and the objective against which the asset is being evaluated.

Attribution for the billboard work described here was also less robust than attribution available for related branded digital activity. Improved measurement is providing more useful directional evidence, but the case should not be read as a controlled demonstration of causal effectiveness.

Experience note. This Insight is based on billboard work from my current role for a multi-location service business. The organization, locations, brand name, campaign details, creative assets, and commercially sensitive performance information have been generalized. Part I focuses on the attention and viewing-context problem; Part II continues the same line of inquiry by examining what helps a brand remain recognizable after the exposure ends.
Research use note. The research referenced here examines roadside visual attention, driving performance, processing fluency, and socially transmitted visual attention. None of those studies validates The Billboard Glance Test. The framework is my own applied synthesis. The driving studies support the importance of limited attention and exposure conditions; they do not establish that my billboard treatment was commercially superior. The processing-fluency literature provides a useful way to think about ease of decoding, but it does not establish that simpler creative will always outperform more complex creative. The crowd-attention research demonstrates that other people's gaze can influence visual attention in public environments; it does not demonstrate that group viewing improves advertising recall, comprehension, or conversion.
