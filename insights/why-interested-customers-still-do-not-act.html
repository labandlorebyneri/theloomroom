Why interested customers still do not act

A framework for diagnosing friction between intention and conversion.

By Neri Published 14 September 2026 Applied lifecycle + performance marketing
Tags Lifecycle Marketing · Performance Marketing
Framework The Friction Audit

Interest is not the same thing as action.

A customer can understand an offer, believe it is relevant, and even intend to proceed—then leave without converting. When that happens, the instinct is often to increase persuasion: strengthen the headline, add urgency, show more proof, or drive more traffic.

Sometimes that is the right response. Sometimes the customer is already persuaded enough.

The short answer

A conversion problem is not always a persuasion problem. When interest already exists, the more useful diagnosis may be friction: unclear next steps, excessive effort, unresolved uncertainty, too many choices, poor timing, missing assistance, or weak follow-up. The job of marketing may be to make action easier rather than to argue harder.

This builds on the same logic behind Why the problem matters more than the first idea: diagnose the barrier before choosing the intervention.

01

Context

High intent can still end in no action.

Conversion journeys often contain visitors who have already done meaningful cognitive work. They searched, clicked, read, compared, or returned to a page because something about the offer mattered to them.

Yet interest can still stop short of action.

Psychology has long documented a gap between intention and behavior. A major meta-analysis by Webb and Sheeran found that experimentally induced changes in intentions produced smaller changes in subsequent behavior, reinforcing the point that stronger intention does not automatically translate into equivalent action.1

That distinction matters in marketing because conversion metrics are often treated as if they reveal persuasion alone.

They do not.

A person may be willing to act and still hesitate because the next step feels effortful, uncertain, badly timed, overly complex, or difficult to navigate.

The absence of conversion does not prove the absence of intent.

02

Problem

Teams often try to persuade harder when the journey itself is creating resistance.

If a landing page underperforms, common responses include rewriting the headline, increasing urgency, adding testimonials, launching retargeting, or expanding traffic acquisition.

Those interventions assume the main barrier is motivation or belief.

But decision-making also carries costs. A recent interdisciplinary review describes cognitive costs as burdens that can arise at different stages of decision-making and influence whether and how people proceed.2 Experimental work also supports the broader observation that cognitively demanding options can be experienced as costly and may be avoided, although those evaluations depend on context.3

That does not mean every extra form field or paragraph creates a measurable psychological penalty. It does mean that effort belongs in the diagnosis.

If a customer has to interpret an unclear offer, search for basic information, decide among too many pathways, wait for reassurance, or guess what happens after clicking, the journey is asking them to solve additional problems before they can act.

At that point, more persuasion may simply be layered on top of unresolved friction.

03

What I observed

The bottleneck can sit between a customer's question and the next available action.

One ongoing experiment in my work has made this especially tangible.

Anonymized ongoing experiment

For a client in a high-consideration professional-services category, I am testing the use of an AI-assisted chat widget on selected high-intent website pages, including a priority conversion landing page.

The working hypothesis is not that visitors necessarily need more promotional messaging. Some may already have sufficient interest but still carry a question, uncertainty, or hesitation that interrupts the path to inquiry.

The widget is therefore being evaluated as a potential conversion interface: a way to make relevant assistance available closer to the moment friction appears.

This is intentionally an experiment, not a success story.

There is no responsible basis yet for claiming that the intervention has increased conversion. The value of the test is that it turns a broad question—how do we get more conversions?—into a more specific one:

Can timely conversational assistance reduce a meaningful source of friction for high-intent visitors?

04

What I did

I treated the conversion path as a system to diagnose.

I use a seven-part framework I call The Friction Audit.

1. Clarity

Does the customer understand the offer, the relevance, and what happens next?

2. Effort

How much work—cognitive or practical—is required before the customer can progress?

3. Trust

What uncertainty, perceived risk, or credibility concern could make action feel premature?

4. Timing

Is the call to action appearing when the customer is ready for that level of commitment?

5. Choice

Are there too many competing pathways, decisions, or calls to action?

6. Assistance

Can the customer resolve a question or uncertainty at the moment it appears?

7. Follow-up

If the customer does not act immediately, is there a useful path back into the journey?

For the AI-assisted chat experiment, that framework changes the implementation question from Should we add a chatbot? to Which friction, if any, is the chatbot supposed to reduce?

The test therefore needs to consider placement, trigger behavior, conversation design, escalation to human support, conversion pathways, and measurement—not merely whether people open the widget.

Because the use case involves a high-consideration service, responsible deployment also requires clear boundaries. Automated assistance should not create the impression that a user has received individualized professional advice when they have not, and the journey should provide a clear route to human support where appropriate.

05

Why I chose that approach

Reducing friction is a different strategic task from increasing motivation.

The intention-behavior gap is useful because it prevents a common assumption: that if people are not acting, they must not want the outcome strongly enough.

Sometimes they do.

The problem can emerge later, during execution of the decision.

Research on cognitive costs gives me another useful lens: effort can arise during different stages of decision-making and can influence whether and how people proceed.2 I do not treat that as proof that every extra step is harmful. It is a reminder that effort belongs in the diagnosis.

Choice also requires nuance. More options are not inherently harmful, but research on choice overload shows that difficult choice environments can sometimes produce avoidance, regret, or dissatisfaction, with effects moderated by both the decision context and characteristics of the decision-maker.3

For marketing, my interpretation is straightforward:

Do not remove steps, choices, or information blindly. Remove the friction that does not help the customer make a better decision.

06

What happened

The experiment is still in progress, so the result is a question—not a claim.

I would not publish a conversion lift, engagement gain, or ROI claim before the evidence exists.

At this stage, the experiment is useful because it creates a more disciplined measurement agenda.

The questions include:

Usage: Do high-intent visitors actually engage with conversational assistance?

Need: Which questions or uncertainties repeatedly appear?

Timing: Where in the page journey does interaction occur?

Progression: Does engagement with assistance correlate with stronger movement toward a meaningful inquiry?

Page fit: Do some high-intent pages benefit more than others?

Escalation: When does automated assistance need to hand off to a person?

Incrementality: Does the intervention appear to reduce friction, or does it merely add another interface?

The answers should determine whether the widget is expanded, redesigned, restricted to specific contexts, or removed.

This distinction later became central to Why the lead is not the conversion: interest or inquiry is only one transition in a larger conversion system.

The technology is not the strategy. The experiment exists to determine whether the technology addresses a diagnosed barrier.

07

What this taught me

Conversion optimization should begin with the reason action is difficult.

It is tempting to treat conversion as the final proof of persuasion.

But the journey between interest and action contains its own behavioral demands.

A customer may need to understand what happens next.

They may need reassurance before sharing information.

They may need fewer competing choices.

They may need an answer at the moment a question appears.

They may need a human conversation rather than another automated message.

Or they may simply need the journey to require less unnecessary work.

That changes the optimization question from:

How do we make people want this more?

to:

If they already want to move forward, what is making the next step harder than it needs to be?

08

How I would apply it elsewhere

Audit the path from intention to action before adding more persuasion.

I would use the same diagnostic across landing pages, forms, ecommerce journeys, email programs, lead funnels, event registration, onboarding, CRM workflows, and other conversion paths.

Clarity: Does the audience understand the value and next step?

Effort: Are there unnecessary steps, fields, searches, or decisions?

Trust: What unresolved risk or uncertainty could prevent commitment?

Timing: Are we asking for too much too early—or failing to make the action available when intent peaks? That question also connects to Why the best call to action depends on where the customer is in the journey.

Choice: Does the interface help people choose, or simply present options?

Assistance: Can questions be resolved through useful content, conversational support, or human contact?

Follow-up: If someone leaves, is there a sensible way to continue the journey later?

Only after that would I decide whether the intervention should be stronger persuasion, a different message, a simplified experience, better follow-up, human support, conversational assistance, or something else entirely.

The broader lesson

Do not confuse willingness with ease.

Conversion happens when several conditions align.

The customer has to see enough value to consider acting. But the journey also has to make the action understandable, credible, timely, and manageable.

That is why more traffic, more urgency, more copy, or more persuasion cannot solve every conversion problem.

Sometimes the audience does not need another reason to act.

They need fewer reasons not to.

When people already have intent, the strategic opportunity may be to reduce the distance between “I want to” and “I did.”

Experience note. The AI-assisted chat example is based on an ongoing experiment in my current work for a high-consideration professional-services business. The organization, pages, market, implementation details, and performance data are anonymized. No conversion lift or other performance outcome is claimed because the test is still in progress.
Research use note. The sources below support the intention-behavior gap, cognitive costs in decision-making, and the context-dependent effects of choice overload. They do not validate the Friction Audit or prove that AI-assisted chat reduces conversion friction. The Friction Audit and its application to this case are my own synthesis.
