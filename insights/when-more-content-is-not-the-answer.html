When more content is not the answer

A framework for diagnosing whether the content problem is really about volume, relevance, distribution, differentiation, or attention.

By Neri Published 13 September 2026 Applied marketing strategy + behavioral science
Tags Content Marketing · Content Development & Strategy · Channel Strategy
Framework The Content Constraint Check

Publishing more is one of the easiest responses to a content problem. Engagement falls, so the calendar gets fuller. Traffic slows, so more articles are commissioned. Social performance softens, so posting frequency increases.

The instinct is understandable: if content creates visibility, then more content should create more visibility. But content competes for something that cannot simply be produced on demand: attention.

The short answer

More content is not the answer when volume is not the constraint. If performance is being limited by weak relevance, poor distribution, low differentiation, journey friction, or limited attention, increasing output can simply create more underperforming assets. Diagnose the constraint before increasing production.

This applies the same diagnostic principle I use more broadly in Why the problem matters more than the first idea: start with the barrier, not the tactic.

01

Context

Content volume is visible. Content effectiveness is harder to diagnose.

Content teams work inside systems that reward production. There are calendars to fill, search topics to cover, social channels to maintain, campaigns to support, emails to send, and audiences expected to stay engaged.

When performance weakens, increasing output can feel productive because it is immediate and measurable. Another post can be published. Another article can be written. Another video can be produced.

But publishing activity and audience response are different variables. Research on selective attention begins from a basic constraint: the brain cannot process all incoming information equally, so attention prioritizes a subset of available input for deeper processing.1

The audience does not experience a content calendar. They experience individual moments in which your message competes with everything else asking for attention.

Another piece of content is not another guaranteed interaction. It is another opportunity to compete for selection.

02

Problem

More information does not automatically create more usefulness.

When content underperforms, volume can become an attractive explanation: we are simply not publishing enough.

But the content may not address a meaningful audience need. The right asset may exist but receive weak distribution. The brand may be covering too many disconnected topics to build a clear association. The format may be poorly matched to the channel. Or the audience may consume the content without seeing a logical next step.

Research on information overload is relevant here. Reviews describe overload as a condition in which information demands exceed a person's capacity to process them effectively, although the mechanisms and consequences vary by setting.2

That does not mean a large content library automatically overloads customers. It does mean that more information is not inherently better information.

If the problem is relevance, another irrelevant post does not solve it. If the problem is differentiation, another interchangeable article may reinforce it. If the problem is distribution, producing something new while useful assets remain unseen may simply create more underused inventory.

The content gap has to be identified before it is filled.

03

What I observed

Repetition can help, but repetition is not the same thing as redundancy.

Content strategy contains an apparent contradiction. Brands need repetition because a single exposure rarely creates a durable association. At the same time, repeating the same execution indefinitely does not guarantee an endlessly increasing response.

The psychology of repeated exposure helps make that distinction more precise. A large meta-analysis of the mere exposure effect found that repeated exposure and liking were not related in a simple linear way; across the studies reviewed, the pattern was consistent with an inverted-U relationship under many conditions.3

I would not treat either finding as a direct rule for posting frequency. The more useful strategic interpretation is narrower:

Repeat the strategic signal when repetition is useful. Vary the execution when identical repetition adds little.

One proposition can become an article, short-form video, case example, visual explanation, customer story, email, search answer, event conversation, or sales enablement asset. The strategic idea remains coherent while the audience experience changes.

04

What I did

I diagnose the content system before adding to it.

My working rule across content, search, social, email, and campaign planning is simple: treat “we need more content” as a hypothesis to test, not an instruction to accept automatically.

I use a six-part diagnostic I call The Content Constraint Check.

1. Purpose

What job is the content supposed to perform: awareness, explanation, credibility, search discovery, consideration, conversion, retention, or something else?

2. Audience

What real question, tension, decision, or need does the content help the intended audience resolve?

3. Distinctiveness

Does the content give people a reason to associate the idea with this brand rather than with the category in general?

4. Distribution

Are strong assets actually reaching the intended audience through search, social, email, paid amplification, partnerships, or other channels?

5. Journey

What should happen after someone consumes the content, and is that next step obvious, credible, and easy?

6. Inventory

What already exists, and what can be refreshed, consolidated, repackaged, redistributed, internally linked, or retired before something new is created?

This keeps content marketing and channel strategy connected. The content determines what is being communicated; the channel helps determine where, when, and under what conditions it has a chance to be processed. I later developed that channel-side question further in Why being on every channel is not a channel strategy.

05

Why I chose that approach

Attention is a selection problem before it is a production problem.

Every piece of content enters a competitive environment. Search results, feeds, inboxes, recommendations, notifications, conversations, and offline experiences all contain other signals competing for selection.

Selective-attention research supports a general principle that is useful here: limited processing capacity means some information receives priority while other information receives less processing.1

For strategy, that makes relevance, salience, timing, context, and distribution as important as production.

A search article can earn attention because it closely answers an active question. An email can earn attention because it arrives at a relevant stage of the customer relationship. A case study can earn attention because it reduces uncertainty for someone evaluating a decision. A social post may first have to create enough salience to interrupt scrolling.

So I do not ask only, Did we publish?

I ask, Why would this be selected, understood, remembered, and acted on?

06

What happened

The content question becomes more precise.

Once content is evaluated as a system instead of a quota, “we need more content” breaks into more useful possibilities.

More coverage may be necessary because important audience questions remain unanswered.

Better distribution may be the priority because strong assets exist but too few relevant people encounter them.

Better differentiation may matter because the output is useful but indistinguishable from competitors.

Better sequencing may be required because individual pieces exist without guiding people through a journey.

Better repurposing may create more value because strong ideas are being abandoned after one format or one publication.

Better consolidation may outperform expansion because several weak pieces could become one stronger resource.

The diagnosis does not automatically lead to less content. It leads to more intentional content.

07

What this taught me

Consistency should mean consistency of value, not simply frequency.

Brands need repetition, but repetition works best when it reinforces something worth remembering.

The mere-exposure evidence does not support the simplistic idea that each additional exposure produces unlimited additional liking.3

My takeaway is not “do not repeat yourself.” It is:

Repeat the strategic idea without requiring the audience to experience the same execution every time.

A strong content system can explain a principle, demonstrate it, show a case, answer an objection, visualize it, connect it to a current question, turn it into a tool, and adapt it to another stage of the journey.

The brand becomes more coherent without every interaction becoming interchangeable.

Consistency is not the number of times a brand publishes. It is the clarity of what the audience repeatedly learns to associate with it.

08

How I would apply it elsewhere

Before producing another piece, identify which constraint needs to move.

I would work through the same sequence:

Is there an actual content gap?
If an important audience question or journey stage has no useful content, create it.

Is there an attention problem?
If the message is not being noticed, examine format, timing, targeting, creative salience, and channel context.

Is there a relevance problem?
If people see the content but do not care, revisit the audience need and value proposition.

Is there a distribution problem?
If good assets exist but few people encounter them, improve discovery and amplification before expanding inventory. In search, that same distinction between producing content and making it discoverable became central to Why ranking is no longer the whole search strategy.

Is there a differentiation problem?
If the content resembles everything else in the category, strengthen the perspective, evidence, voice, or distinctive brand cues.

Is there a journey problem?
If people engage but do not progress, clarify the next step and connect content across the decision process.

Is there a repetition problem?
If the strategic signal needs reinforcement, vary the execution rather than abandoning the idea—or repeating an identical treatment indefinitely.

Only after those questions would I ask:

Do we actually need more?

The broader lesson

Content is valuable because of the job it performs.

Content may help someone notice a brand, understand an idea, answer a question, remember a proposition, reduce uncertainty, compare options, trust an organization, or take the next step.

More content can increase the number of opportunities for those things to happen. But it can also create duplication, noise, fragmented attention, and a larger library of assets that nobody meaningfully encounters.

The objective is not maximum output.

Create the right signal for the right audience, in the right context, often enough to become meaningful—without mistaking repetition for strategy.

Before adding another post to the calendar, another article to the backlog, or another asset to the campaign, I would ask:

What is the content system currently failing to do?

Sometimes the answer is more content.

Sometimes the answer is to make the content you already have more relevant, more distinctive, more connected, and easier to find.
