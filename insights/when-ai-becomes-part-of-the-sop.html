When AI becomes part of the SOP

How human-computer interaction can make marketing work faster, more consistent, and more productive without replacing strategic judgment.

By Neri Published 17 September 2026 Applied marketing operations + human-AI workflow design
Tags SOP Writing · Content Development & Strategy · Performance Marketing
Framework The Human-AI SOP Loop

I lead the broader organic and creative strategy in my current role. The scope stretches across content, search, email, community initiatives, editorial planning, creative review, research, performance analysis, and quality control. You can see more of that work on my Experience page.

The number of tasks that can cross my desk is overwhelming for one person.

But we get it done.

The secret is not that AI replaced the strategy.

It became a support system around the strategy.

The short answer

AI creates the most value for me when it is embedded into a repeatable workflow: the human defines the objective, AI assists with a bounded task, the human reviews the output, strategic decisions remain human-owned, and the process improves over time. At that point, AI is no longer just a tool I occasionally open. It becomes part of the SOP.

01

Context

The AI question is often framed too broadly.

Marketing conversations about AI frequently begin with a large question: What can AI replace?

I find a smaller question much more useful:

At which point in this workflow can AI reduce unnecessary effort without removing the judgment the work depends on?

That change in framing matters because marketing is not one task. It is a chain of different cognitive and operational activities.

Some work involves monitoring. Some involves information gathering. Some involves synthesis. Some involves drafting. Some involves checking. Some involves prioritization. And some requires contextual judgment, tradeoffs, accountability, and a final decision.

Human-factors researchers Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens proposed that automation can operate at different levels across functions such as information acquisition, information analysis, decision and action selection, and action implementation.1 Their work was not about generative AI or marketing, but the distinction is useful: automation does not have to be all-or-nothing.

That is how I approach AI in my own work.

I do not ask it to become the marketing strategy. I decide where it can support the system.

02

Problem

Using AI without a process can create more output without creating better work.

AI makes it easy to produce something quickly.

That is useful, but speed alone does not solve the marketing problem.

A fast draft can still be strategically wrong. A summary can omit context. A recommendation can sound plausible while being poorly supported. A research output can surface weak sources. A content audit can identify a signal without understanding why it matters.

The risk is that a team adopts AI first and then tries to decide what role it should play.

I prefer the reverse.

Start with the workflow. Identify the bottleneck. Define what needs human judgment. Then decide whether AI belongs in one part of the process.

This is the same diagnosis-before-tactic logic I explored in Why the problem matters more than the first idea: choosing the tool is easier after the actual constraint has been defined.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework similarly emphasizes that human roles and responsibilities in decisions involving AI should be clearly defined and differentiated.2

The goal is not maximum automation. The goal is a better-designed human-computer workflow.

03

What I observed

The workload became more manageable when I stopped treating AI as one general-purpose assistant.

Because I lead the broader organic and creative strategy, a large number of different tasks can require attention at the same time.

Strategy still has to be developed. Campaigns still have to move. Content needs to be planned. Creative needs review. Search performance needs attention. Research has to be checked. Processes have to be documented. Data has to be interpreted. Work has to be prioritized, revised, coordinated, and eventually shipped.

I still own those decisions.

What changed was the amount of cognitive and operational effort required to move from one stage of the work to another.

I began decomposing the workload into narrower support functions and assigning AI a specific role inside each one.

Instead of one AI system trying to be strategist, researcher, writer, analyst, and reviewer at once, I built several specialized workflows around recurring jobs.

AI does not remove the marketer from the workflow. It allows the marketer to spend more of the workflow on the parts that actually require a marketer.

04

What I did

I turned recurring AI use into a role-based support system.

Internally, I gave several AI workflows names inspired by Abbott Elementary characters. The names are mnemonic devices: they make the functions easier for me to remember, distinguish, and route work toward.

The names are playful. The jobs are not.

The operating principle

Each role has a bounded function, defined inputs, an expected output, and a human review point. None of them owns the marketing strategy or final decision.

Janine Teagues

Email marketing support

Janine supports the drafting of major email campaigns and a recurring community newsletter. She works from instructions I provide and from a pool of recommendations informed by performance analysis.

Human owns: objective, audience, message direction, prioritization, and approval.

AI assists with: synthesis, structure, drafting, and iteration.

Mr. Johnson

News sweeper

Mr. Johnson scans the daily news environment for developments relevant to priority service topics, including legislation and other changes that may affect the subjects we cover.

Human owns: relevance threshold, editorial judgment, and whether the development warrants action.

AI assists with: monitoring, discovery, and preliminary filtering.

Gregory Eddie

Research and source support

Gregory works quietly in the background once a topic has been identified. He summarizes, evaluates relevance, pulls facts, and surfaces sources worth reviewing.

Human owns: interpretation, source judgment, and strategic relevance.

AI assists with: research organization, evidence discovery, and synthesis.

Tariq

Calendar and opportunity monitoring

Tariq tracks relevant awareness periods, celebrations, community events, and other calendar opportunities that could matter to the marketing program.

Human owns: campaign selection, relevance, timing, and resource allocation.

AI assists with: calendar monitoring, opportunity discovery, and advance notice.

Jacob Hill

Content validation and quality control

Jacob helps evaluate and revalidate factual claims in drafted long-form content. The workflow also checks for potential content cannibalization, Google E-E-A-T considerations, SEO fundamentals, answer-engine readability, generative-search discoverability, and whether factual claims still have adequate support.

Human owns: the article, the argument, final claims, corrections, and publication decision.

AI assists with: validation, risk detection, structured QA, and review prompts.

O'Shon

Performance triage

O'Shon runs a data-driven review of top-performing and at-risk webpages using analytics reporting. The purpose is to surface pages that deserve additional support and pages showing deterioration that should be investigated before the problem becomes larger and harder to correct.

Human owns: diagnosis, intervention, prioritization, and strategic response.

AI assists with: analysis, pattern detection, and prioritization signals.

Seen together, the system divides the workload into specialized cognitive functions:

Monitor → news, events, performance signals

Research → facts, evidence, source discovery

Draft → first-pass structured outputs

Validate → factual, search, and quality checks

Prioritize → identify where attention may be most valuable

Decide and direct → human

I did not use AI to build an artificial marketing department. I used it to build a support layer around a human-led marketing function.

05

Why I chose that approach

A repeatable AI workflow needs the same discipline as any other SOP.

The shift happened when I stopped treating repeated prompting as an informal habit and started treating it as process design.

If AI is used only when someone happens to remember to open a chatbot, it is a tool.

If the workflow deliberately specifies where AI assists, what context it receives, what output is expected, what must be checked, and what decisions remain human-owned, it has become part of the SOP.

I think about the process through a simple framework:

The Human-AI SOP Loop

Define→ Assist→ Review→ Decide→ Execute→ Learn

Define. The human establishes the objective, context, constraints, inputs, and success criteria.

Assist. AI performs a bounded task such as monitoring, synthesis, drafting, analysis, organization, or first-pass validation.

Review. The human checks accuracy, context, omissions, assumptions, relevance, risk, and tone.

Decide. Strategic judgment stays with the accountable human.

Execute. Approved work moves into the real marketing process.

Learn. The SOP is refined based on what created value, what failed, what needed correction, and where the AI layer did or did not reduce effort.

This is compatible with a broader human-factors principle: automation can support different functions at different levels rather than replacing the entire human role.1

The question is not whether AI belongs in marketing. The question is where it belongs in the workflow, under whose judgment, and with what controls.

06

What happened

The support layer expanded what I could process without transferring strategic ownership.

I am deliberately not attaching a fabricated productivity percentage to this system.

The practical effect is simpler: recurring work that once required me to start from zero can now arrive in a more usable state.

News can be surfaced before I decide whether it matters. Potential topics can arrive with preliminary research before I decide whether they deserve development. Email work can begin from a structured draft rather than a blank page. Long-form content can go through an additional validation pass before publication. Analytics can be triaged so I can investigate the pages most likely to need attention instead of manually treating every page as equally urgent.

The gain is not just output volume.

It is the ability to allocate more of my own attention to prioritization, synthesis, creative direction, interpretation, and decisions.

The AI layer expands the amount of information and work I can process. It does not inherit the authority to decide what the marketing strategy should be.

07

What this taught me

Productivity is not the same as producing more AI output.

A workflow is not more productive simply because more text, more reports, or more recommendations are generated.

The useful measure is whether the human-AI system helps produce more useful work, preserve or improve quality, surface risks earlier, reduce avoidable repetition, and protect human attention for the decisions that need it.

That also means human review cannot be ceremonial.

NIST notes that human-AI interaction can produce different outcomes depending on the configuration, and that human roles and responsibilities need to be clearly differentiated.2 For me, that is an operating principle rather than a compliance slogan.

If the human simply accepts the output, the system has not preserved judgment.

If AI produces work that is never checked because the workflow has become too fast, the efficiency gain may be false.

If an AI recommendation changes a strategic priority, I still need to understand the evidence and make the decision myself.

The same boundary appears in Why the lead is not the conversion, where AI-assisted conversation is treated as an ongoing experiment rather than as proof that automation should replace human judgment.

Augmentation works when the machine absorbs bounded effort and the human remains responsible for context, judgment, and consequence.

08

How I would apply it elsewhere

Start with the SOP, not the model.

I would not begin by asking a team to identify every place where generative AI could be inserted.

I would begin with a recurring workflow and document it.

Trigger: What event starts the work?

Owner: Who is accountable for the outcome?

Inputs: What information does the task require?

AI role: Which bounded activity can be assisted?

Expected output: What should AI return, and in what format?

Human review: What must be verified, challenged, or approved?

Decision boundary: What is AI not authorized to decide?

Next action: Where does the approved output go?

Feedback: What should be changed in the SOP when the workflow fails or improves?

This structure can apply to content, reporting, research, lifecycle marketing, campaign planning, operations, or other repeated marketing activities.

The AI component may change as tools change. The operating logic should be more durable.

A good AI SOP should survive a change in model because the process is built around the work, not around the novelty of the tool.

The broader lesson

AI works better as infrastructure than as strategy.

The most useful change in my own practice was conceptual.

I stopped asking AI to be everything.

I gave it jobs.

Some jobs monitor. Some research. Some draft. Some validate. Some analyze.

I still decide what deserves attention, what the evidence means, what the strategy should be, what is safe to publish, and what the organization should actually do.

That division of labor is what makes the system useful to me.

It also makes the role of an SOP more important, not less.

Once AI becomes part of daily human-computer interaction, the workflow needs boundaries, inputs, review points, ownership, and learning mechanisms just like any other operational process.

AI should not replace the marketing strategy. It should make the human-led marketing system more capable of executing it.

Scope

This is an operating model, not a universal staffing prescription.

This Insight is based on my own workflow as the person leading a broad organic and creative marketing function with a high volume of recurring work.

It should not be read as evidence that one marketer plus AI should replace a properly staffed team, nor as a recommendation that every marketing organization structure work this way.

Workload, risk, regulation, brand complexity, customer needs, organizational maturity, available expertise, and the consequences of errors all change what should remain human-led and what can be safely assisted.

The character-based workflow names are internal mnemonic devices inspired by Abbott Elementary. They are not an affiliation with or endorsement by the program or its rights holders.

The transferable idea is not the names. It is the discipline of assigning AI a bounded role inside a documented human-owned process.
