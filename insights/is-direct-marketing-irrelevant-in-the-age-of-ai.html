Is direct marketing irrelevant in the age of AI?

What repeat clients, referrals, and visible effort taught me about maintaining relationships after the original service ends.

By Neri Published 26 September 2026 Applied direct marketing + lifecycle thinking
TagsDirect Marketing · Lifecycle Marketing
FrameworkThe Relationship Effort Loop
The short answer

No. AI can make communication faster, easier to personalize, and easier to automate. But direct marketing still depends on knowing who you are speaking to, why you are speaking to them, and what relationship already exists.

In my current work, repeat clients and referrals from former clients are among our highest-converting sources. I cannot attribute that result to any one retention activity, but it makes the continued relationship commercially important enough to study.

01

Context

Direct marketing in my current role does not begin with a cold list.

It begins with people who already know us.

For the multi-location service business where I currently work, former clients are invited into a dedicated Facebook group. They are also invited to register for an annual gift card that is mailed directly to their home.

Neither activity is built around pretending the previous relationship never happened. The opposite is true.

The starting point is that a relationship already exists. The marketing question is whether that relationship should simply end when the original service does.

That made me reconsider what direct marketing means in an environment where AI can generate messages, segment audiences, recommend next actions, and personalize communication at increasing scale. Industry work on AI-enabled marketing increasingly describes those capabilities: using customer data and AI to tailor communications and orchestrate more individualized experiences.7

But making communication easier to produce does not automatically make relationships easier to create.

02

Problem

If communication becomes effortless, what still makes direct marketing valuable?

AI makes it increasingly possible to generate large amounts of tailored communication.

That can make direct marketing look almost old-fashioned. Why spend time maintaining known relationships when technology can continuously identify new audiences and produce personalized messages for them?

My current work gives me a reason not to accept that conclusion.

Repeat clients and referrals from former clients are among our highest sources of conversion.

That is an internal business observation, not a controlled experiment.

It tells me that existing relationships have commercial value. It does not tell me why those sources convert strongly, and it does not prove that our Facebook community or annual mailed gift-card program caused the result.

The conversion data tells me the relationship has value. It does not yet tell me which part of our relationship marketing created that value.

03

What I observed

Repeat and referral business arrive with a different starting condition.

A repeat client has previous experience with the organization.

A referred prospect often arrives through someone who has previous experience with the organization.

In both cases, the relationship begins with information that a cold acquisition channel has to work harder to establish: prior knowledge, remembered experience, or another person's recommendation.

Research on customer referral programs provides some support for why that distinction can matter. Schmitt, Skiera, and Van den Bulte followed roughly 10,000 customers of a German bank for almost three years and found that referred customers had higher retention and greater customer value than comparable nonreferred customers in that setting.1

Later work by Van den Bulte and colleagues identified two mechanisms that can help explain such differences: better matching between the referred customer and the firm, and social enrichment created by the continuing connection to the referrer.2

Those studies do not establish that every referral is more valuable. They also do not establish that our own referral performance comes from the same mechanisms.

But they make the pattern plausible enough to investigate rather than dismiss.

04

What I did

I started thinking about the system as a Relationship Effort Loop.

The Relationship Effort Loop
Experience→Community→Recontact→Small Commitment→Visible Effort→Recall→Repeat / Referral

Experience — The original service creates the first memory of the business.

Community — Former clients can remain connected through a dedicated Facebook group.

Recontact — The relationship has legitimate opportunities to continue after the original engagement.

Small Commitment — A former client who wants the annual gift card chooses to register.

Visible Effort — The organization administers the program and sends the gift directly to the person's home.

Recall — The business has another opportunity to be remembered outside the original service interaction.

Repeat / Referral — When another relevant need appears, the former client may return or may recommend the business to someone else.

The sequence is not intended as a proven causal model. It is a way for me to describe the relationship we are trying to maintain and the points where customer effort and organizational effort meet.

05

Why I chose that approach

The psychology of effort is more useful when we distinguish whose effort we are talking about.

It would be easy to misuse behavioral research here and conclude that making customers work harder will make them value the relationship more.

I do not think the evidence supports that as a general marketing rule.

Research on service encounters has repeatedly found that customers notice provider effort. Mohr and Bitner found that perceived employee effort had a strong positive relationship with satisfaction even after statistically accounting for the success of the service outcome.3

More directly relevant to the balance between both sides, Söderlund and Sagfossen experimentally manipulated supplier effort and consumer effort. Higher perceived supplier effort increased satisfaction, while consumer effort produced either no direct benefit or an indirect negative effect. Satisfaction was also higher when customers perceived that the supplier had expended more effort than they had.4

The useful principle is not “make the customer work.” It is “let the customer perceive that the relationship was worth some effort from us.”

The annual gift-card program illustrates that distinction.

The former client has to do something small: register if they want to participate.

After that, the organization carries most of the process. The program is administered and the gift is delivered directly to the client's door.

That is a very different experience from creating artificial inconvenience and asking the customer to interpret the inconvenience as value.

Research on the effort heuristic provides a related but narrower lens. Kruger and colleagues found that people sometimes judged work as more valuable or higher quality when they believed more effort had gone into producing it, particularly where quality was ambiguous.5 I use that finding cautiously here. Their experiments were not customer-retention programs, and they do not show that mailing a gift card will automatically make a business more valued.

What they do reinforce is a simpler idea:

Effort can itself become information about how seriously someone treated an interaction.

06

What happened

Repeat clients and referrals became one of our strongest conversion sources—but I cannot attribute that result to one program.

The commercial pattern is clear enough for me to pay attention to it.

For the multi-location service business, repeat clients and referrals from former clients are among the highest sources of conversion.

The attribution is much less clear.

Former clients who join the Facebook group or register for the annual gift may already be more satisfied, more engaged, more connected to the organization, or more likely to recommend it.

That creates an obvious selection problem.

The same underlying relationship quality could explain both participation in these activities and later repeat or referral behavior.

Without a comparison group, longitudinal attribution, or a controlled experiment, I cannot say:

The Facebook group caused the repeat conversion.

Or:

The mailed gift caused the referral.

Research does, however, provide mechanisms that make continued relationship investment worth examining.

Palmatier and colleagues used both a laboratory experiment and a longitudinal field study and found that perceived relationship-marketing investments generated gratitude, which in turn was associated with reciprocal behaviors and performance outcomes including purchase intentions, sales growth, and share of wallet.6

A controlled field experiment by Beltramini also randomly assigned customers to receive or not receive a business gift and found attitudinal and behavioral-intention effects from the gift within the marketing program studied.8

Neither study tests our Facebook group. Neither tests our annual gift-card program. Neither proves why our repeat and referral sources convert strongly.

So I treat the current evidence as:

Observed commercial pattern+Plausible relationship mechanisms+Unresolved attribution

That is more useful to me than pretending correlation has already answered the question.

07

What this taught me

Convenience should reduce the customer's work without erasing evidence of ours.

This changed the way I think about “friction.”

Marketing often treats convenience as the removal of every possible action.

But there is a difference between unnecessary friction and a small act of participation.

Registering for the annual gift provides an active signal: the former client still wants to participate. That also connects to Why the best call to action depends on where the customer is in the journey: the appropriate next action for a former client may be a small act of continued participation rather than another immediate sales ask.

The strategic mistake would be turning that small commitment into a burden.

We do not need to make the form longer, the process harder, or the gift more difficult to obtain in the hope that effort will create attachment.

The better design is the opposite:

Keep the customer's effort proportionate. Make the organization's follow-through meaningful.

This also connects to Why the lead is not the conversion. Conversion is not a single moment. What happens before and after a transaction can change the probability that the relationship continues.

And it connects to When AI becomes part of the SOP. AI can support segmentation, messaging, timing, and operational consistency. It can help scale personalization. It can make the system around the relationship more efficient.7

But:

AI can make a message effortless to produce. Relationship marketing still benefits from giving people evidence that the relationship itself was worth some effort.

08

How I would apply it elsewhere

Start with the relationship before deciding what to automate.

For former customers, I would ask:

What did they experience with us?

Do we have a legitimate reason to remain in contact?

What information, community, or benefit would make that continued relationship useful?

What small action would appropriately signal that they still want to participate?

What should the organization do after receiving that signal?

And can repeat or referral outcomes be measured in a way that separates correlation from actual incremental impact?

That final question matters.

If the relationship program matters commercially, eventually it deserves better measurement.

A future analysis could compare eligible former clients who participate with those who do not, while accounting for obvious differences between the groups. A stronger design could examine changes over time, test different retention touchpoints, or use randomized components where ethically and operationally appropriate.

Until then, the right conclusion is not that the program caused the outcome.

It is that the outcome gives us a strong reason to understand the relationship better.

The broader lesson

Direct marketing is not becoming irrelevant. Its most valuable input may be the relationship that already exists.

AI can help decide who receives a message.

It can help generate the message.

It can help determine timing, segment audiences, and personalize at scale.

But a former client who returns or refers another person is not responding to technology in the abstract.

They are responding from a history with the business.

The Facebook community and annual mailed gift are two ways we currently choose to continue that history. The same continuity principle appears in Why community events should solve a relationship problem: sometimes the marketing job is not immediate conversion, but creating a useful reason for the relationship to continue.

I cannot say that either one created our repeat and referral conversion performance.

I can say that repeat clients and referrals are important enough in our conversion mix that the post-service relationship should not be treated as an afterthought.

The end of one customer journey can become the beginning of another.

AI can help us decide when and how to reopen a relationship. Direct marketing still depends on having a relationship worth reopening.

Scope

What this case does—and does not—show.

This case establishes an internal business pattern: repeat clients and referrals from former clients are among the multi-location service business's strongest sources of conversion.

It does not establish that participation in the Facebook group or annual gift-card program causes repeat business or referrals. Participants may differ systematically from nonparticipants, and other aspects of the original client experience may be responsible for both continued engagement and subsequent advocacy.

The Relationship Effort Loop is therefore an applied model for thinking about the program, not a validated causal model.

Experience note. This Insight is based on my current work for a multi-location service business. The organization, markets, client records, conversion figures, referral volumes, gift-card details, and other commercially sensitive information have been generalized. No individual client information is disclosed.
Research use note. The research cited here comes from banking, retail, service encounters, experiential offers, relationship marketing, and business-gift contexts. I use these studies as evidence for plausible mechanisms—not as proof that the same effect occurs in our specific program. The Relationship Effort Loop and its application to this case are my own synthesis.
