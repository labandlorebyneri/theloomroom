What makes a brand easier to remember?

What repeated exposure, distinctive brand cues, color, and visual continuity teach us about building recognition over time.

By Neri Published 21 September 2026 Applied brand strategy + behavioral science
Tags Brand Marketing · Asset Development · Campaign & Advocacy Development
Framework The Brand Memory Loop

Yesterday, I wrote about a billboard designed for people who might give it only a moment of attention.

The creative became unusually simple: one dominant color, one brand name, almost nothing else.

That solved one problem.

It reduced the amount of information competing for attention.

But it exposed a second problem:

Getting noticed once is not the same as becoming recognizable later.

A brand is rarely built in one exposure.

It is built as people encounter the same name, colors, typography, imagery, language, and ideas across time.

The short answer

A brand becomes easier to remember when repeated exposures reinforce a sufficiently distinctive and sufficiently consistent set of cues. Color, typography, language, imagery, names, and other assets can all contribute, but none works as a universal shortcut. The strategic task is to decide which cues should remain stable enough to accumulate recognition—and when a meaningful change is worth the memory cost of teaching the audience something new.

01

The problem after attention

Once the audience sees you, what helps them know it is you the next time?

In Part I, I argued that OOH effectiveness begins with the objective and the amount of attention available in the viewing environment.

For an awareness-led billboard, reduction can make sense because the creative job may be as simple as:

Notice → Recognize → Remember

But the word remember contains a much larger Brand Marketing problem.

What exactly is being remembered?

The logo?

The color?

The name?

The person?

The category?

A phrase?

And what happens when those cues change?

That question became much more concrete for me in another role, where I worked on the visual identity of a public-facing professional.

02

A visual identity can carry momentum—and changing it can interrupt that momentum.

The existing identity centered on green and blue.

As the individual entered a new, higher-profile stage of public-facing work, we transitioned the visual system toward orange and electric blue.

The new palette served a strategic purpose. It helped distinguish a new chapter of the brand and gave the new campaign a more distinctive visual system of its own.

But the change came with a trade-off.

The previous colors had already accumulated exposure.

People who had encountered the earlier identity were being asked to connect a new set of cues with a person they may already have learned through the old ones.

The transition disrupted some of the momentum of the previous visual identity even as it helped establish the next one.

I would not claim that the palette change caused a particular public or electoral outcome. The evidence does not support that causal statement.

The useful lesson was about something more fundamental:

When a brand changes its cues, the audience has to learn the change too.

That is true far beyond public figures.

A company rebrand, product repositioning, merger, new service line, packaging redesign, new spokesperson, or category expansion can all create the same tension.

The change may be strategically necessary.

But it is rarely cognitively free.

03

Familiarity is built through exposure, but repetition is not a magic formula.

The classic mere-exposure literature provides one useful lens.

Zajonc's foundational work proposed that repeated exposure to a stimulus can influence attitudes toward it.1

A later meta-analysis by Bornstein found that exposure effects varied with conditions including stimulus type, complexity, exposure duration, recognition, and number of presentations.2

That caveat matters.

I would not translate mere exposure into:

Show the same thing enough times and people will automatically like the brand.

That is too strong.

The more useful strategic interpretation is that repetition can create familiarity, but what the audience becomes familiar with depends on which cues are being repeated.

If every campaign changes its palette, typography, imagery, tone, and brand treatment at the same time, the organization may be buying repeated exposure without repeatedly teaching the same recognition pattern.

Repetition only accumulates brand memory when something recognizable survives the repetition.

04

What I use instead: the Brand Memory Loop

The Brand Memory Loop
Exposure→ Recognition→ Association→ Repetition→ Retrieval

Exposure

Did the audience actually encounter the cue?

A brand asset cannot build memory if it is rarely seen, inconsistently applied, or buried beneath the campaign execution.

Recognition

Can the audience identify that they have encountered this before?

This is why consistent color, typography, naming, imagery, or visual structure can matter. The goal is not to make every asset identical. The goal is to preserve enough continuity that the audience does not have to solve the brand again from zero.

Association

What does the cue become connected to?

Recognition without meaning has limited value.

A color may become familiar, but ideally it becomes familiar as part of something: a service category, a reputation, a person, an experience, a point of view, or a specific promise.

Repetition

Are later encounters reinforcing the same memory structure?

This is where consistency becomes strategic rather than decorative.

Brand consistency does not mean repeating the same layout everywhere. As I argued in Why being on every channel is not a channel strategy, different channels have different jobs and behavioral conditions.

The social asset, billboard, email, website, and event collateral can all look different in format while repeating recognizable brand cues.

Retrieval

Can the brand come back to mind when the relevant need appears?

This is the outcome that makes brand memory strategically useful.

Recognition during an exposure is valuable. But the stronger commercial question is whether the brand can become mentally available later—when the person is searching, comparing, asking for a recommendation, or facing the problem the brand is positioned to solve.

05

Color works best as a learned cue, not a universal code.

Color is one of the easiest parts of branding to oversimplify.

Red means urgency.

Blue means trust.

Green means growth.

Those statements are too blunt to function as reliable strategy.

Labrecque and Milne's research examined how hue, saturation, and value can influence brand perceptions and demonstrated that color can affect judgments such as brand personality, familiarity, liking, and purchase intent.3

That does not create a universal dictionary where one color has one fixed meaning.

Category, culture, prior exposure, competitive context, saturation, brightness, surrounding colors, and the rest of the brand system all influence interpretation.

For me, the more useful question is not:

What does orange mean?

It is:

What are we repeatedly teaching the audience to associate with this orange?

That distinction matters in both of the applied cases behind these two Insights.

In the billboard case from Part I, a dominant red field helped reduce the number of cues competing with the brand name.

In the public-facing identity case, changing from green and blue to orange and electric blue gave the next stage a more differentiated look—but it also required rebuilding familiarity around a new visual pattern.

Color therefore operates as both an attention device and a memory device.

Those jobs are related, but they are not identical.

Use color not only to attract attention today, but to create a visual pattern people can recognize tomorrow.

06

Familiar things can become easier to process.

Processing fluency adds another useful piece to the puzzle.

Reber, Schwarz, and Winkielman reviewed evidence showing that factors such as perceptual clarity, repetition, familiarity, symmetry, and figure-ground contrast can affect how easily something is processed.4

Again, I would not convert that into a simplistic rule that familiar branding is always better or that novelty is harmful.

Novelty can be strategically powerful.

The issue is what the novelty is replacing.

If a new visual system is introduced, the audience may initially need more effort to connect it with the existing brand.

That is not necessarily a reason to avoid the change.

It is a reason to recognize that a rebrand creates a learning period.

A transition plan can therefore preserve selected cues—such as the name, face, tone, symbol, or messaging—while changing others.

The objective is not always maximum continuity.

It is enough continuity to help the audience bridge old and new when that bridge is strategically useful.

07

Primacy and recency are useful prompts, not billboard-placement laws.

The serial-position effect is often summarized as better memory for items appearing early and late in an ordered sequence. Murdock's classic free-recall work is one of the foundational demonstrations of that pattern.5

But that research involved sequences of information, not brand layouts.

So I would not say that the top or bottom of a design automatically receives a memory advantage.

I use primacy and recency more cautiously as two questions:

What is the first meaningful brand cue the audience encounters?

and

What is the final useful cue likely to remain before the exposure ends?

That can apply to a billboard, carousel, video, landing page, email, speech, or campaign sequence.

If the brand appears only after the audience has already processed everything else, recognition may depend on people staying long enough to reach it.

If the first cue establishes the brand and the final cue reinforces it, the identity is less dependent on complete exposure.

This is a design prompt—not a claim that serial-position research directly predicts advertising performance.

08

When should a brand preserve its cues—and when should it change them?

This is where the public-facing identity case became most useful to me.

The old visual system had familiarity.

The new visual system had a strategic job.

Neither fact automatically answered whether the transition was right.

I now think about that decision through five questions:

Recognition — What existing association might be weakened?

Distinctiveness — Does the new system separate the brand more clearly from its competitive or cultural environment?

Continuity — Which existing cues can remain so the audience has a bridge between old and new?

Meaning — Does the change correspond to a real change in positioning, audience, offering, stage, or strategy?

Repetition — Will the organization have enough future exposure to teach the new system?

The cost of change is not evidence that a brand should never change.

Sometimes a break is exactly the point.

The new identity may need to signal a new ambition, audience, category, or phase.

But when that happens, the organization should understand that it is not only changing a design.

It is changing what the audience has been learning to recognize.

How I would apply it elsewhere

Audit the cues before redesigning the brand.

If I were evaluating a brand system before a redesign, I would not begin by asking which palette looks more current.

I would first inventory the cues already doing memory work.

Which colors appear consistently?

Which typography is recognizable?

Which phrases are repeated?

Which people, symbols, shapes, or visual treatments recur?

Which of those cues are actually associated with the brand rather than simply existing in a brand guide?

Then I would ask what the new system needs to accomplish.

Does it need greater distinctiveness?

A clearer category signal?

A stronger connection to a new audience?

A cleaner cross-channel system?

A visible break from the past?

Only then would I decide which cues should remain, which should evolve, and which should disappear.

This is also why brand consistency should not be confused with sameness. I explore that distinction more directly in Why a template is not a brand, where the question shifts from memory cues to how a brand system can remain recognizable across different executions.

A billboard can reduce the system to one dominant color and a name.

A social asset can use two brand colors, imagery, supporting copy, and a CTA.

A website can introduce additional hierarchy and interaction.

The layouts can differ while the recognition structure remains coherent.

The broader lesson

Attention gets the brand into the moment. Consistency helps the brand survive beyond it.

Part I started with a billboard and a question about attention.

How much can someone process before the board is already behind them?

Part II ends with a different question.

What happens after the exposure is over?

A distinctive asset can help a brand get noticed.

A repeated asset can help it become familiar.

An associated asset can help it mean something.

And a retrievable brand can become available when the audience eventually needs it.

That is why the job of brand identity is larger than visual consistency.

The system has to make repeated encounters accumulate.

A brand becomes easier to remember when future exposures reinforce what previous exposures have already taught the audience to recognize.

Sometimes the right strategy is to preserve those cues.

Sometimes the right strategy is to change them and deliberately build a new memory structure.

The important part is knowing which problem you are solving.

Scope

What these cases do—and do not—show.

The primary applied example in this Insight comes from work on a public-facing professional's visual identity. Names, organizations, locations, campaign titles, political details, and other identifying information have been generalized.

The transition from one palette to another should not be interpreted as evidence that specific colors caused a public, campaign, or electoral outcome.

The case is useful because it illustrates the strategic tension between preserving accumulated recognition and creating a new visual identity for a new stage.

The billboard example referenced from Part I has likewise been anonymized and should not be read as evidence that any specific color or minimalist layout universally improves brand memory.

Brand memory depends on many factors beyond visual identity, including reach, frequency, category cues, experience, reputation, messaging, audience relevance, competitive context, and the consistency with which the organization actually deploys its assets.

Experience note. This is Part II of a two-part Insight. Part I examined attention and viewing context through an anonymized OOH case; this second part examines what happens after exposure, using an anonymized visual-identity transition from another role. Research use note. The research referenced here examines mere exposure, exposure effects, color and brand perceptions, processing fluency, and serial-position memory. None of those research streams directly validates The Brand Memory Loop or the applied brand-transition framework used here; those are my own strategic synthesis. Mere exposure does not mean repetition automatically creates liking or commercial performance. Color research does not establish universal meanings for individual colors. Processing fluency does not mean familiar creative always outperforms novelty. The serial-position effect comes from memory research involving ordered information, so primacy and recency are used here as prompts for thinking about communication sequence—not as evidence that physical positions in a brand asset automatically receive a memory advantage.
