Why the best call to action depends on where the customer is in the journey

How progressive commitment changed the way I think about what to ask a lead to do next.

By Neri Published 23 September 2026 Applied lifecycle marketing + lead nurture
Tags Lifecycle Marketing · Email & SMS Marketing · Content Marketing
Framework The CTA Readiness Ladder
The short answer

A call to action should not simply reflect what the business ultimately wants. It should reflect what the customer appears ready to do next. Someone downloading an educational resource has demonstrated interest. They have not necessarily demonstrated readiness for a high-commitment conversation. The strongest CTA is therefore not always the biggest ask. It is the next reasonable step for the customer at that point in the journey.

01

Context

Someone downloaded a practical guide. Technically, that made them a lead.

It did not tell me what they were ready to do next.

I am currently building a nurture journey for a professional-services business where people enter after downloading a practical guide related to a high-stakes personal problem.

The resource meets the customer at an information-seeking stage. They may still be trying to understand the problem, their options, or whether professional assistance is necessary.

The download tells us the problem matters enough to investigate. It does not tell us they are ready for direct professional assistance.

As I explored in Why the lead is not the conversion, raising a hand is evidence of interest. It is not the same thing as completing the customer journey.

What should we ask them to do now?

02

Problem

Marketing often asks for the final action too early.

Book the consultation. Request a quote. Start the application. Schedule the call.

Those actions make sense from the organization's perspective because they sit close to the business outcome. The customer may still be several steps away.

A person downloading an informational guide may still be asking: What are my options? Do I need professional help? Do I trust this organization enough to talk to someone?

A CTA that ignores those unresolved questions can ask for more commitment than the relationship has earned.

This is where the customer journey becomes more useful than the button copy.

03

What I observed

Different behaviors reveal different levels of readiness.

The nurture workflow I am building does not move directly from:

download → consultation

Instead, the initial guide provides practical value. Follow-up helps the person keep evaluating the problem. A self-assessment tool adds structure. A conversational experience creates room for questions. Direct professional assistance remains available when the person appears ready for a higher-commitment step.

The progression looks more like:

Practical guide→ Follow-up→ Self-assessment→ Conversation→ Conversion opportunity

Each step asks slightly more from the customer: more attention, more information, more interaction, more commitment.

That made me realize that CTA strategy is not simply about writing more persuasive verbs.

It is about deciding how much commitment the next step should require.

04

What I did

I began thinking about the sequence as The CTA Readiness Ladder.

The CTA Readiness Ladder
Interest→ Value→ Evaluation→ Interaction→ Commitment

Interest — The customer has shown that the problem matters enough to investigate.

Value — Give them something useful before demanding significantly more.

Evaluation — Help them understand their situation more clearly.

Interaction — Create an opportunity for a deeper exchange.

Commitment — Ask for the higher-intent action when behavior provides a stronger reason to believe they may be ready.

This does not mean every customer must climb every rung. Someone with an urgent need might move directly into professional assistance; someone else may need several interactions.

The framework is not a rigid funnel. It asks:

What is the next reasonable commitment given what this person has already done?

That is different from asking what action would be most valuable to us.

05

Why I chose that approach

The psychology of progressive commitment offers a useful explanation.

One relevant concept is the foot-in-the-door technique.

In classic experiments, Freedman and Fraser found that agreeing to a relatively small initial request could, under certain conditions, increase compliance with a larger subsequent request.1

I would not translate that research into a marketing rule that says: make people click something small and they will eventually convert.

That is too strong.

The experiments concerned sequential requests in particular settings. A modern nurture journey also involves need, trust, urgency, perceived risk, timing, and service quality.

But the research gives me a useful lens:

Commitment does not always have to begin with the largest request.

Sometimes a smaller, meaningful action creates a more appropriate bridge to the next one.

06

What happened

The workflow now treats behavior as information.

The campaign is still active, so I do not yet have a completed performance story to tell.

What has changed already is the design logic.

A person who interacts with follow-up communication may be showing something different from someone who remains inactive. A self-assessment may indicate deeper involvement than the original download, while beginning a conversation may provide stronger evidence of readiness for a direct next step.

That means the workflow can respond to both where the customer broadly sits in the journey and what their recent behavior suggests about readiness.

Two people who downloaded the same resource on the same day do not necessarily deserve the same CTA a week later.

07

What this taught me

A CTA is part of the customer journey, not decoration added at the end of a message.

When reviewing email, landing-page, or nurture copy, I still ask whether the CTA is clear, specific, and useful.

But there is an earlier question:

Is this the right action to ask for at all?

A beautifully written CTA can still be strategically wrong if the customer is not ready for what it requests.

That connects with Why being on every channel is not a channel strategy. A channel needs a defined role in the journey.

So does a CTA.

It also connects with Why interested customers still do not act. Sometimes the customer does not need more persuasion. They need a clearer, easier, or more appropriate next step.

08

How I would apply it elsewhere

Match the size of the ask to the evidence of readiness.

Customer statePossible next action
Early-stage interestLearn more
Active evaluationAssess your situation
Seeking clarificationAsk a question
Stronger intentExplore your options
Ready for direct helpSpeak with someone

Those are not universally correct CTAs. The point is the progression.

The ask should become more consequential as evidence of readiness becomes stronger.

The final business objective may stay the same while the appropriate next action changes throughout the journey.

The broader lesson

The customer does not experience a funnel as a diagram.

They experience a series of decisions.

At each stage they are deciding whether the next action feels useful, relevant, and worth the effort.

That makes a call to action more than a conversion device. It is a proposition:

Given what you know and feel right now, is this next step worth taking?

The business already knows where it ultimately wants the customer to go. Good lifecycle marketing pays equal attention to where the customer is starting from.

The best CTA is not necessarily the action closest to conversion. It is the action that makes the next stage of the relationship reasonable.

Scope

Where this framework applies—and where more evidence is needed.

The CTA Readiness Ladder is an applied framework for thinking about nurture journeys and progressive customer actions. It does not imply that every journey should contain the same number of steps or that smaller requests always improve eventual conversion.

Urgency, prior knowledge, customer intent, service complexity, trust, channel, and the quality of the offer can all change what constitutes a reasonable next action.

Experience note. This Insight draws from an active lead-nurture campaign I am developing for a professional-services business. The journey begins with a practical guide related to a high-stakes personal problem and continues through follow-up, self-assessment, conversational support, and opportunities for direct professional assistance. The organization, specialization, market, technology stack, audience size, and performance data have been generalized. The campaign remains in progress, so this article documents strategy and decision logic rather than a completed performance outcome.
Research use note. The foot-in-the-door research examines sequential compliance requests under controlled conditions. I use it as a conceptual lens for progressive commitment, not as evidence that increasingly consequential marketing CTAs will necessarily increase conversion. The CTA Readiness Ladder is my own applied framework.
