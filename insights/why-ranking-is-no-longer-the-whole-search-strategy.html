Why ranking is no longer the whole search strategy

A framework for building visibility across traditional search, answer engines, and generative discovery.

By Neri Published 15 September 2026 Applied search strategy + behavioral science
Tags SEO / AEO / GEO Marketing & Strategy · Content Development & Strategy · Channel Strategy
Framework The Search Visibility Stack

For years, one of the clearest ways to describe search performance was position.

Where do we rank? Are we on page one? Did the target query move up or down?

Those questions still matter. Ranking has not become irrelevant. Google explicitly says that foundational SEO remains relevant to its generative AI features because AI Overviews and AI Mode are rooted in core Search ranking and quality systems.1

But ranking is no longer the whole picture.

The short answer

Ranking remains important, but modern search visibility cannot be evaluated through rank position alone. A useful search strategy also asks whether content can be accessed, matched to the right intent, understood, trusted, differentiated, surfaced across relevant discovery experiences, and ultimately connected to meaningful business outcomes.

This is the same diagnostic principle behind Why the problem matters more than the first idea: the metric that is easiest to see is not always the whole problem.

01

Context

Search is becoming a discovery system, not only a ranked list of links.

Traditional search trained marketers to think vertically: a user enters a query, the engine returns ranked results, the website competes for position, and the user chooses a page.

That sequence still matters. It now operates alongside additional discovery behaviors.

Google describes AI Overviews as a way to help people get the gist of complex questions and AI Mode as particularly useful for exploration, reasoning, and comparison. Both can use query fan-out, where the system issues multiple related searches across subtopics and retrieves additional supporting pages before generating a response.2

Microsoft is exposing a similar expansion through Bing Webmaster Tools. Its AI Performance reporting shows citation activity in AI-generated answers, including cited pages and grounding queries, while newer reporting adds signals such as intents, topics, and citation share.4

The practical implication is not that SEO disappeared.

The unit of search visibility has expanded beyond a single query-position pair.

02

Problem

Ranking can tell you where a page appears without telling you the full role it plays.

A keyword ranking is useful because it gives us an observable signal. If a priority page moves materially upward for a commercially important query, something meaningful has changed.

But ranking becomes incomplete when it is treated as the objective rather than one indicator of discoverability.

Microsoft describes the distinction directly: traditional search is optimized around which pages a user should visit, while AI grounding asks what information can responsibly support a generated response. The underlying infrastructure overlaps, but the retrieval objective is different.5

Google, meanwhile, cautions against treating AEO or GEO as a separate collection of tricks. Its current guidance says optimizing for generative AI search on Google is still grounded in SEO fundamentals, including technical accessibility, useful content, and core Search systems.1

The problem is therefore not that ranking stopped mattering.

It is that a ranking report can be narrower than the discovery environment we are trying to influence.

03

What I observed

High visibility does not necessarily mean strong search performance.

A recent analysis I conducted for a high-consideration legal-services client made this distinction especially clear.

Anonymized real-world analysis

I reviewed roughly 16 months of Google Search Console performance for a legal-services website. Client, market, query, and URL details are intentionally withheld. The figures below are rounded from the internal analysis.

The objective was not simply to identify where the site ranked. It was to understand how existing organic visibility could produce more commercially valuable visits and ultimately more qualified opportunities.

~3.74M Google Search impressions across the analysis window
~9.1K Clicks generated from that visibility

The primary challenge was not that Google could not see the site. It was already receiving meaningful exposure, including for commercially valuable service-related searches.

But many of the highest-clicked pages were informational, branded, community, team, or navigational. The pages and queries most closely aligned with potential customer acquisition were capturing only a small portion of their available visibility as traffic.

One priority service page was particularly revealing. It was already appearing around positions 4 to 7 for important high-intent searches, yet click volume remained very low.

If I had looked only at position, I could have concluded that the page was already performing reasonably well.

The relationship between position, impressions, clicks, URL selection, commercial intent, and the eventual business outcome told a different story.

The site did not simply have a ranking problem. It had a search visibility efficiency problem.

This also connected with a useful concept from Peter Pirolli's Information Foraging Theory: information scent. In Web-foraging research, information scent refers to cues people use to judge whether following a link or information path is likely to lead toward useful information.6

I do not treat information scent as a Google ranking theory. I use it as a human-behavior lens.

Ranking affects exposure. The search result still has to signal to the person searching: this appears relevant, this understands my need, and this is worth continuing with.

04

What I did

I evaluated search performance as a stack instead of reducing it to rankings.

The analysis led me to formalize a seven-layer diagnostic I call The Search Visibility Stack.

1. Access

Can search systems crawl, index, and technically access the priority page?

2. Relevance

Is the right page being associated with the right intent, topic, location, and query cluster?

3. Answerability

Does the page—and the search result representing it—clearly communicate that it can solve the searcher's problem?

4. Evidence

Can the claims, expertise, and information on the page withstand scrutiny and support a high-consideration decision?

5. Distinctiveness

Why should a person or retrieval system select this source instead of another credible result?

6. Discovery

Across which search or AI-assisted discovery surfaces could this information reasonably appear?

7. Outcome

What meaningful behavior should that visibility create—qualified traffic, calls, inquiries, registrations, leads, assisted conversions, or another business objective?

The stack helped separate what the evidence showed from what I might otherwise have assumed.

Access: visibility showed that discovery was already happening.

Millions of impressions made one point clear: basic organic access was not the principal constraint. Google was already surfacing the site at significant scale.

That did not prove every technical element was perfect. It did mean that “Google cannot find us” would have been an inadequate explanation of the commercial opportunity.

Relevance: the right topic was not always resolving to one clear destination.

For one major local commercial topic, the intended service page was generally recognized as the primary result. But the homepage and secondary URLs were also receiving visibility for the same commercial searches.

That suggested URL fragmentation: the site could rank for the topic, but Google was receiving competing signals about which page should represent that intent.

The resulting action plan included clearer page differentiation, stronger internal linking, reinforced geographic relevance, review of overlapping URLs, and stronger signals establishing the intended commercial landing page as the preferred destination.

I later returned to this same body of search work in Why search intent matters more than keyword volume alone, this time asking what the searcher may have been trying to accomplish rather than only where the site appeared.

Answerability: the searcher still has to choose the result.

The priority page that was already appearing around positions 4 to 7 demonstrated why ranking alone was insufficient.

If the page is visible but scarcely clicked, the search-result presentation becomes part of the diagnostic.

That led to recommendations to test title tags and meta descriptions around direct hiring intent, strengthen the search-result value proposition, improve above-the-fold commercial messaging, and align the promise made in the search result with the page the visitor reaches.

Search optimization does not end when the algorithm chooses to display the page. The searcher still has to choose it.

Evidence: analytics show behavior, but not every reason behind it.

Search Console can tell me where impressions and clicks occur. It cannot independently tell me whether a factual claim is convincing, whether an attorney profile creates trust, or whether the page answers the customer's uncertainty well enough to support a decision.

So I treat quantitative search data as one input and evaluate the page itself separately.

Analytics can show where behavior changes. They cannot always tell us why.

Distinctiveness: visibility creates competition for selection.

Once a service page is already appearing among several credible options, keyword inclusion is not the entire decision problem.

The question becomes: Why should someone choose this result?

That is where search begins to overlap with positioning, brand, customer understanding, and conversion strategy.

Google's current guidance for generative AI search also emphasizes valuable, expert-led, non-commodity content rather than material that merely repeats information already available everywhere.1

Discovery: not every visibility layer is measurable through the same tool.

The client analysis was based on Google Search Console data for conventional organic search performance. I would not use that dataset to claim the pages were being cited in AI-generated answers.

That evidence requires different reporting.

Google now provides a dedicated Generative AI performance report in Search Console for visibility within generative AI features such as AI Overviews and AI Mode, and says the report rolled out worldwide by August 31, 2026.3 Bing Webmaster Tools separately reports citation activity in supported AI-generated experiences.4

The principle is simple:

Do not make a claim about one discovery surface using evidence from another.

Outcome: the search metric has to connect back to the business objective.

The internal project did not define success as “improve average position.”

Its measurement framework separated SEO leading indicators—including impressions, clicks, CTR, average position, priority-query rankings, cluster-level click capture, and URL overlap—from the business outcomes the firm ultimately cared about.

Those outcomes were qualified organic opportunities and signed business from the highest-priority service categories.

The same logic continues after the click. In Why the best call to action depends on where the customer is in the journey, I apply that journey question to what a visitor should reasonably be asked to do next.

That is where the Search Visibility Stack ends for me.

Not at ranking. Not at impressions. Not even at traffic.

At the business behavior the visibility was intended to influence.

05

Why I chose that approach

Search now contains more retrieval paths than a single keyword-position model can describe.

Google says AI Mode and AI Overviews can use related fan-out searches to retrieve supporting information, while its generative AI features continue to rely on foundational Search ranking and quality systems.12

Microsoft, meanwhile, is explicitly separating the optimization problem of traditional page ranking from the problem of grounding AI-generated answers in supportable information.5

That makes search visibility increasingly multi-layered.

But I would resist the opposite mistake too.

It would be easy to declare that rankings no longer matter and replace one oversimplification with another.

Google's guidance does not support that conclusion. It says core SEO practices remain foundational for generative AI search and that special tactics such as `llms.txt`, artificial content chunking, or rewriting specifically for AI are not required for Google Search.1

So I see SEO, AEO, and GEO less as isolated disciplines and more as different perspectives on one increasingly complex discovery environment.

06

What happened

The diagnosis changed the optimization priority.

The most important result of the analysis was not a new keyword list.

It was a different explanation of the problem.

The site did not need to create search demand from zero. Google was already exposing the business to meaningful commercial search demand.

The immediate questions became:

Can we capture more clicks from visibility that already exists?

Can we reduce competing URL signals?

Can we strengthen the relationship between query intent and the page Google selects?

Can we make the search result more compelling to the person deciding which result to open?

And does the additional traffic create more qualified opportunities for the business?

This led to a phased action plan rather than a generic instruction to “do more SEO.” Initial priorities focused on the highest-value commercial pages and included search-result messaging, above-the-fold commercial relevance, internal linking, geographic signals, and overlapping URLs. Secondary service opportunities followed after the first optimization cycle.

Had I looked only at rankings, I would have missed part of the opportunity. Had I looked only at impressions, I might have mistaken exposure for success.

07

What this taught me

AEO and GEO should expand SEO thinking, not replace SEO fundamentals.

Whenever a new search technology appears, there is a temptation to create a new checklist.

Add an AI file. Rewrite everything into tiny answer blocks. Produce a page for every possible long-tail variation. Chase mentions. Add special markup.

Google's current generative AI guidance pushes back on several of those assumptions. It says Search does not require `llms.txt`, special AI markup, artificial content chunking, or content rewritten specifically for generative AI. It also emphasizes useful, unique, people-first material over scaled commodity content.1

That brings the strategy back to more durable questions.

Can the page be crawled?

Does it address a real information need?

Is the intended destination clear?

Can the claim be trusted?

Does the content contribute something distinctive?

Can relevant retrieval systems find and use it?

Does the resulting visibility create value?

The future of search strategy looks less like optimizing for one metric and more like making useful information reliably discoverable across an evolving information system.

08

How I would apply it elsewhere

Audit discoverability as a system.

For another website, brand, or content program, I would work through the Search Visibility Stack in order.

Access: Are the priority pages crawlable, indexable, canonical, internally linked, and technically healthy?

Relevance: Which audience questions and search intents are genuinely important, and is the right page being selected for them?

Answerability: Does the page clearly resolve the problem it claims to address, and does the search-result presentation communicate that relevance?

Evidence: Are factual claims supported? Is authorship clear? Are sources, dates, experience, and expertise represented accurately?

Distinctiveness: What does the page contribute that is difficult to replace with a generic summary?

Discovery: Which traditional and AI-assisted search surfaces could reasonably expose this information, and which tools can actually measure each one?

Outcome: What behavior would make the visibility commercially or strategically worthwhile?

Traditional rankings remain part of the dashboard.

They simply no longer have to explain everything by themselves.

The broader lesson

Ranking tells us one way information becomes visible.

Search has always been about retrieval.

What is changing is the form of the retrieval experience.

Sometimes the user still chooses from a ranked list.

Sometimes a search system summarizes several sources.

Sometimes it retrieves multiple subtopics before presenting an answer.

Sometimes the person continues the conversation instead of submitting another conventional query.

But the underlying challenge remains recognizable: there is an information need, there is a body of available information, and there is a system trying to connect the two.

The Search Visibility Stack came from seeing a site with millions of impressions where the highest-value opportunity was not simply “rank higher.” It was to understand why existing visibility was not becoming enough clicks, qualified visits, leads, and business outcomes.

That is why I would not abandon ranking.

I would put it in its proper place.

When someone—or a system acting on their behalf—needs an answer, are we one of the sources worth finding?
