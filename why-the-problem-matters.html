Why the problem matters more than the first idea

A framework for diagnosing the marketing problem before choosing the tactic.

By NeriPublished 12 September 2026Applied marketing strategy + behavioral science
TagsCampaign & Advocacy Development · Channel Strategy · Content Development & Strategy
Based onReal-world work + behavioral science

Good marketing rarely begins with What should we make? It begins with a harder question: What actually needs to change?

A campaign can be beautifully executed, distributed through the right channels, and supported by strong creative work and still fail if it is solving the wrong problem. Marketing operates within a system of attention, perception, motivation, judgment, and action. Before choosing a format, channel, message, or creative direction, the strategist has to understand what is preventing the audience from moving from the current state to the desired one.

The short answer

The first idea should not determine the strategy. Diagnose the current state, desired change, audience, and barrier first. Then choose the message, channel, creative execution, or other intervention that addresses that constraint. A tactic can be well executed and still fail when it is solving the wrong problem.

The idea comes later. Diagnosis comes first.

01

Context

Every marketing problem arrives inside a frame.

Marketing teams are often asked to move quickly. Engagement falls, leads slow down, a competitor becomes more visible, or leadership wants stronger growth. The natural response is to start producing: more content, more ads, a new campaign, a refreshed visual system.

But the way a problem is presented can shape the solutions that seem reasonable. Tversky and Kahneman showed that different formulations of the same decision problem can produce predictable shifts in preference, demonstrating the influence of framing on judgment.1

In marketing, the frame may already be embedded in the request. “We need more content” frames the challenge as production. “We need advertising” frames it as awareness. “We need better engagement” may frame it as creative.

Before accepting that frame, I reconstruct the context: What is the business objective? Who needs to respond? What behavior needs to change? What has already been tried? Where in the customer journey is the breakdown?

A marketing request is not always the same thing as a marketing problem.

02

Problem

A familiar solution can make the real problem harder to see.

The visible problem is often only a symptom. Low engagement does not necessarily mean a brand needs more content. Poor conversion does not automatically mean the call to action is weak; sometimes the real issue is the friction between interest and action. Declining search visibility does not necessarily mean more articles need to be published.

A useful parallel comes from the Einstellung effect. Bilalić and colleagues found that a familiar solution can continue to bias attention even when people are trying to search for alternatives.2 I use that finding as an analogy for a marketing risk: familiar tactics can narrow the questions a team considers before the underlying problem is established.

Engagement is down? Post more. Traffic is down? Publish more. Sales are slow? Run a promotion. Awareness is weak? Buy media.

Those tactics may eventually be appropriate. But familiarity is not evidence. The same symptom could reflect audience mismatch, weak positioning, poor distribution, creative fatigue, technical problems, low trust, unclear value, or friction after the click.

A familiar tactic should be treated as a hypothesis, not a diagnosis.

03

What I observed

The same outcome can come from different behavioral mechanisms.

One of the most useful habits in strategy is separating the symptom, cause, and constraint. Instead of asking only what happened, I ask under what conditions it happened.

Audience: Who is responding, and who is not? Attention: Is the message being noticed? Perception: What does the audience think the offer means? Motivation: Is there a meaningful reason to act? Friction: What makes action difficult? Channel: Is the message appearing in an environment suited to the desired behavior? Timing: When does the audience encounter it? Measurement: Are we tracking the behavior that matters?

A person may not convert because they do not understand the offer. Another may understand it but distrust it. Another may trust it but see no urgency. Another may intend to act but encounter too much friction.

The outcome can look identical while the underlying problem is completely different.

04

What I did

I worked backward from the behavior that needed to change.

Current state → desired state → barrier → strategic response → execution → measurement

If awareness is low, the intervention may genuinely be exposure: reach, salience, PR, partnerships, search visibility, social distribution, or out-of-home advertising. But if awareness is already high and conversion is weak, increasing impressions may accomplish very little. The problem may instead be relevance, trust, risk, an unclear offer, or a difficult next step.

Psychology also documents a gap between intending to act and actually doing so. In a meta-analysis of 94 independent tests, Gollwitzer and Sheeran found that implementation intentions—if–then plans specifying when, where, and how a goal-directed response will occur—improved goal attainment on average.3 I do not treat this as evidence that marketing funnels work like implementation-intention experiments; the useful parallel is that intention and action are distinct stages.

Awareness does not guarantee consideration. Consideration does not guarantee preference. Preference does not guarantee action. Action does not guarantee retention.

The tactic becomes clearer once the behavioral barrier becomes clearer.

05

Why I chose that approach

Different decisions require different kinds of persuasion.

People do not evaluate every message with the same degree of attention. Some decisions involve deliberate comparison and research. Others happen under distraction, habit, emotion, or time pressure.

The Elaboration Likelihood Model provides a useful framework for this distinction. Petty and Cacioppo describe persuasion as varying with the degree of elaboration: under higher elaboration, people scrutinize issue-relevant arguments more carefully; under lower elaboration, simpler cues and associations can play a greater role.4

That changes execution. A high-consideration service may need evidence, authority, explanation, reviews, and reassurance. A billboard may need immediate comprehension and distinctive branding. A social post first has to earn attention. An email may depend on relevance and timing. A landing page may need to make the next action obvious.

This is why I do not treat channels as strategies. SEO, social, email, PR, partnerships, influencer work, and OOH are mechanisms. Their value depends on what they are being asked to accomplish and the conditions in which the audience encounters them.

The channel does not merely distribute the strategy. It changes the conditions under which the strategy is processed.

06

What happened

Measurement became part of the reasoning process.

Diagnosing first creates clarity. Once the problem is more precisely defined, the number of plausible tactics becomes smaller. Each execution can be tested against the same question: Does this address the barrier we identified?

It also changes the role of analytics. Impressions, clicks, rankings, engagement, leads, calls, conversions, retention, and revenue become signals about whether the underlying assumptions were correct.

If a campaign creates reach but not consideration, exposure may be working while the message is not. If people click but fail to convert, acquisition may be working while the landing experience is weak. If customers convert but fail to return, the problem may sit beyond acquisition entirely.

Hypothesis → execution → behavior → evidence → learning → adjustment

When results differ from the prediction, the most useful question is not simply “What should we change?” It is “Which assumption appears to have been wrong?”

07

What this taught me

The first idea should not become the strategy simply because it arrived first.

The most useful research parallel here is the Einstellung effect: familiar solutions can bias the search for alternatives.2 The practical implication is straightforward: a familiar tactic should not become the strategy simply because it is the first solution that comes to mind.

Sometimes that means saying: the brief asks for more content, but the problem is positioning. The team wants more reach, but awareness is not the bottleneck. The company wants more leads, but the real friction exists in follow-up.

The first idea can begin the conversation. Evidence should decide whether it survives it.

08

How I would apply it elsewhere

Transfer the principle, not necessarily the tactic.

When I transfer a lesson from one campaign or organization to another, I try to carry over the underlying mechanism rather than copy the visible tactic.

If a campaign succeeds for one organization, the lesson is not automatically “use the same campaign again.” The better question is: What underlying mechanism made it work?

Did it reduce uncertainty? Increase salience? Strengthen social proof? Clarify the value proposition? Match message complexity to audience involvement? Remove friction? Reach people at a more relevant moment? Improve consistency across touchpoints?

Those principles can transfer even when the industry, audience, channel, creative execution, and offer are completely different.

In a new situation I would ask: What is happening now? What needs to happen instead? Who needs to change? What is preventing that change? What role can marketing realistically play? Which strategic response addresses the barrier? Which execution expresses that strategy best? How will we know whether it worked?

The goal is not to reuse solutions. It is to recognize patterns.

The broader lesson

Diagnose the change before designing the intervention.

Marketing strategy is an attempt to influence a system involving attention, perception, motivation, judgment, and behavior. A channel can increase exposure. A message can change interpretation. Evidence can reduce uncertainty. A journey can reduce friction. A reminder can make an intention easier to act on.

But none of those mechanisms matters unless it addresses the barrier actually preventing the desired change.

So before asking What should we create?, I would ask:

What needs to change in how people perceive, decide, or act—and what is preventing that change now?

The strongest idea is not necessarily the first idea, the most fashionable tactic, or the most impressive execution.

It is the intervention that best fits the problem.
