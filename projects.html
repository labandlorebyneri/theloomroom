Market Research for Word-of-Mouth Strategy

A multi-volume market research and marketing strategy project for a regional multi-location service business, examining how an existing relationship-led audience consumes media and how those patterns could inform word-of-mouth growth, content, channels, search, and measurement.

Research was treated as the beginning of the decision process, not the end. Eleven months of actual performance were later used to determine which recommendations held, which changed, and which still needed testing.

Audience Research Statistical Analysis Content Strategy Channel Strategy Search Strategy Lifecycle Marketing Measurement

Research · Analysis · Strategy · Measurement

The Challenge

Turn relationship-led growth into something marketing could study, test, and improve.

The project began with a practical question: what does an existing relationship-led audience actually watch, read, use, and follow, and which of those patterns are strong enough to influence marketing decisions? The aim was not to manufacture a generic audience persona. It was to build a more disciplined basis for word-of-mouth growth, content, media, search, and measurement.

The Research

163 valid responses became the starting point, not the conclusion.

The survey ran from November 2 to December 3, 2025 and was distributed through established relationship networks. Respondents reported social-platform use, daily social time, streaming behavior, show preferences, sports interests, website categories, and other media habits.

Because the sample was relationship-based rather than a random statewide sample, findings were interpreted as evidence about the recruited audience, not as statewide population estimates.

01

163 valid responses

Every descriptive percentage and later analytical test began with the same anonymized response set.

02

50 reported ZIP codes

Geography described where the recruited responses came from, not market penetration or statewide demand.

03

Multi-select behavior mattered

People could select several platforms, genres, teams, and website categories, so overlapping audiences could not be treated as separate people.

04

Association was not treated as causation

Co-selection patterns were used to generate testable creative and channel hypotheses, not claims about what would automatically perform.

Selected Findings

The useful signal was often in the overlap, not the ranking.

85.3% selected Facebook
66.3% used free ad-supported streaming
77.3% selected Netflix
50.9% selected local news websites
01

Facebook provided broad coverage inside the recruited audience.

139 of 163 respondents selected Facebook. Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube added additional users, but substantial overlap meant platform counts could not simply be added together as unique reach.

02

Heavy social use was common enough to complicate platform interpretation.

The study separated general media intensity from genuine platform preference so highly active respondents would not create false channel-specific conclusions.

03

Local information remained part of the audience's media environment.

Local news websites were selected by 83 respondents, providing a useful signal that local context could matter alongside social and entertainment media.

04

Content affinities were more useful than simple popularity lists.

Supported genre relationships included reality with lifestyle, sports with news, family with lifestyle, and comedy with drama. These became creative hypotheses rather than fixed audience labels.

05

Sports affinity needed careful interpretation.

LSU and Saints following were widespread, while the NFL-to-sports-programming relationship was the team-to-genre pairing that remained supported after correction.

06

Media use did not automatically equal purchasable advertising reach.

Service use, show preference, and self-reported timing were kept separate from measured ad delivery, performance, and conversion outcomes.

From Research to Strategy

Convert findings into decisions without pretending the survey could answer everything.

The strategic output was designed around what the research could responsibly support. Audience evidence shaped content directions, channel roles, local context, search questions, and a measurement approach, while uncertain areas were deliberately turned into tests rather than declarations.

01

Use one core strategy across channels

Adapt format and execution to the platform without fragmenting the brand into unrelated content systems.

02

Build around useful audience moments

Translate interests into recognizable situations and practical content jobs rather than treating a favorite show or team as the strategy itself.

03

Keep local context meaningful

Use community familiarity where it improves relevance, not as decoration added after the message is already finished.

04

Separate audience habits from performance

Survey timing and media use informed hypotheses, but live platform data still had to determine when and where content actually performed.

05

Measure progressively stronger outcomes

Attention and engagement were treated as earlier signals, while downstream response and business outcomes remained the stronger tests of marketing value.

06

Design the strategy to be revisable

Every important recommendation needed a path to later evidence so the strategy could improve rather than harden into an untested rule.

The Lookback

Eleven months later, the original strategy was put back under review.

The research was not treated as complete once the recommendations were delivered. Search, website content, social, paid media, email, CRM, and related execution were later reviewed against the original strategic assumptions. The purpose was to identify what real behavior affirmed, challenged, complicated, or had not yet tested.

Research

Established the audience evidence and the limits of what could responsibly be inferred from a relationship-based survey.

Strategy

Converted observed patterns into content, channel, search, and measurement hypotheses.

Execution

Put those hypotheses into actual marketing work rather than leaving them inside a research deck.

Lookback

Used subsequent performance evidence to determine which recommendations should remain, change, or receive a better test.

Next cycle

Turned disagreements between the survey and observed performance into revised hypotheses instead of treating them as research failure.

The Lesson

A strategy should be allowed to prove itself wrong.

The strongest outcome of the project was not a permanent list of recommendations. It was a repeatable research-to-execution loop: audience evidence creates a hypothesis, execution exposes it to reality, observed behavior changes the decision, and the next test becomes more precise.
