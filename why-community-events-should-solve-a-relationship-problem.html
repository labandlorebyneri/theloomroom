Why community events should solve a relationship problem, not just generate attendance

What familiar activities, useful experiences, and low-commitment interactions taught me about building trust before asking for conversion.

By Neri Published 18 September 2026 Applied community strategy + behavioral science
Tags Public Relations & Events · Partnerships & Collaboration · Campaign & Advocacy Development
Framework The Relationship Entry Ladder

Attendance is easy to count.

People arrived. They registered. They entered the room. They participated. They took home something useful or valuable.

From an event-reporting perspective, those numbers matter.

But attendance can also become a misleading endpoint.

A community event can be full and still do very little to strengthen the relationship between the organization and the people it wants to serve.

The short answer

Community marketing becomes more useful when the first interaction asks relatively little of people, connects with something they already value, and gives them a reason to experience the organization before being asked for a larger commitment. For service businesses and public-facing professionals, that matters because much of what people are evaluating—competence, reliability, judgment, values, trustworthiness—is difficult to inspect in advance.

01

Context

A service is harder to sample than a product.

Many ecommerce businesses can put something relatively concrete in front of a customer. The buyer can inspect the product, compare features, see demonstrations, or purchase a lower-cost item first and decide whether the brand deserves another purchase.

Many service businesses face a different problem.

A prospective customer may not know whether the service is effective until after they have already committed money, time, personal information, or trust.

The same problem can exist for public-facing professionals. People may be evaluating qualities such as judgment, competence, reliability, communication, or values—qualities that cannot always be demonstrated through a conventional product sample.

Services marketing research has long treated intangibility as one of the characteristics that changes how services are evaluated and marketed.1

My practical response has been to create lower-commitment ways for people to experience something about the organization before asking them to make the larger decision.

When the offering itself is intangible, the experience around it can become part of the evidence.

02

Problem

Incentives can generate attendance without necessarily generating a relationship.

In one anonymized service-business setting, several types of in-person community activations had been used over time. Some centered heavily on giveaways and incentives.

They attracted people.

But one recurring, familiar game-based community event consistently produced particularly strong participation from former customers, friends and family, and people from the surrounding community.

There were still prizes, so it would be easy to conclude that people came because they could win something.

But other activations had incentives too.

That made me wonder whether the incentive was only part of the explanation.

The format itself appeared to matter. People already understood the activity. They knew what participation looked like. They could bring other people. There was conversation, anticipation, play, and an obvious shared experience.

The event did not require people to develop an interest in something the organization wanted to promote.

It began with something they already knew how to enjoy.

Understanding what people already enjoy may build a stronger relationship than simply increasing what they can win.

03

What I observed

Familiarity can lower the cost of the first interaction.

A familiar event requires less explanation. People generally know what they are walking into. They understand the rhythm. They know how to participate.

That does not prove they will attend or that familiarity automatically creates trust.

But psychology offers several useful ways to think about why familiar experiences may feel easier to approach.

Robert Zajonc's work on the mere-exposure effect showed that repeated exposure can influence affective responses toward stimuli.2

I do not interpret that body of research as proving that a familiar community activity will outperform an unfamiliar one. My application is narrower: a recognizable experience can remove some of the interpretation required before participation.

For some participants, familiar community activities may also carry personal associations with family gatherings, neighborhood events, schools, churches, clubs, or other shared environments. I treat that as a plausible contextual factor rather than as a psychological effect I can infer from attendance alone.

04

What I did

I started treating community marketing as relationship design.

I began thinking about these experiences through a framework I call The Relationship Entry Ladder.

The Relationship Entry Ladder
Low commitment→ Familiarity→ Experience→ Value→ Trust→ Advocacy

The point is not that every person progresses neatly through every stage. It is a way of asking what the first interaction is actually doing.

Low commitment

The first ask should be proportionate to how much trust already exists.

Come to an event. Read something useful. Ask a question. Participate in an activity. Learn something.

None requires the person to make the highest-commitment decision immediately. That same principle later became more explicit in Why the best call to action depends on where the customer is in the journey.

Familiarity

Does the interaction connect with something the audience already understands?

A recognizable activity lowers the amount of explanation required. Repeated community presence can also help an organization become less abstract.

Recognition is not the same as trust, but it can create the conditions for another interaction.

Experience

This is where an intangible brand begins to become observable.

People can see whether the environment feels welcoming. They can observe how staff behave. They can experience whether the organization feels organized or chaotic. They can decide whether the interaction feels transactional.

For a service organization, the experience surrounding the service can communicate something before the service itself is ever purchased.

Value

The person should receive something meaningful from the interaction even if they do not convert.

Sometimes that value is social. Sometimes it is entertainment. Sometimes it is information. Sometimes it is reassurance.

That became particularly clear to me in a second anonymized experience.

I worked on the strategy for a public-facing professional whose long-term brand depended heavily on familiarity, credibility, and organic word of mouth.

Instead of relying primarily on promotional messaging, we developed accessible educational content around practical problems that could arise in everyday household life.

The topics involved things people could genuinely need help understanding: family responsibilities, property questions, administrative procedures, interactions with institutions, and similar everyday concerns.

If you want people to remember you, give them something useful to remember you for.

The audience did not first have to make a major commitment. They could encounter the information, evaluate whether it was useful, observe how the person explained a complicated subject, and decide whether that experience warranted further attention.

Trust

Trust should not be treated as something an event or article automatically produces.

A pleasant night does not prove that an organization will deliver excellent professional service. Useful educational content does not prove that a public-facing professional will perform well in every future situation.

But these interactions can provide signals.

The organization becomes slightly less unknown. The audience has another piece of evidence on which to form a judgment. Trust can then accumulate—or fail to accumulate—across repeated experiences.

Advocacy

Only after there is something worth talking about does word of mouth become strategically interesting.

A person may tell someone to join the next event, or share useful information they encountered.

That is different from formally recruiting someone as an ambassador. It is advocacy emerging from experienced value. When advocacy becomes a deliberate partnership decision, the problem changes; I explore that separately in Why reach is not enough when choosing an influencer or partner.

Word of mouth becomes stronger when people have something substantive to talk about.

05

Why I chose that approach

A small first interaction can be more useful than a premature large ask.

The classic foot-in-the-door studies by Freedman and Fraser examined whether agreement with a small initial request could increase compliance with a later, larger request.3

I think the concept provides a useful lens here, but I would not use it literally as a marketing formula.

My objective is not: get people to agree to something small so they become easier to persuade later.

That misses the more important part of the interaction.

The first step should give the person information too. They are evaluating us while we are interacting with them.

Create a low-risk first interaction that gives people enough experience to decide whether the relationship deserves another step.

That protects the agency of the audience. It also produces a better marketing discipline.

The event has to be worth attending. The content has to be useful. The experience has to stand on its own.

The first interaction cannot exist only as bait for the second one.

06

What happened

The strongest participation did not appear to come from incentives alone.

In the community-event example, the familiar game-based format repeatedly became one of the strongest gathering points across the in-person activities being used.

That observation does not establish a controlled causal result.

Several factors could contribute: timing, audience composition, promotion, prizes, existing awareness, social behavior, repetition, or the format itself.

So I would not claim that familiarity caused higher attendance.

What I can say is that the pattern changed how I approached the strategy.

It encouraged me to look beyond the incentive and examine the experience people were choosing to participate in.

A giveaway asks: Do you want the reward? A well-designed community experience can also ask: Do you enjoy being here?

The second question is much closer to a relationship question.

07

What this taught me

Likeability can open the door. It cannot carry the entire relationship.

There is a temptation in community marketing to interpret this argument as: make people like you.

That is incomplete.

Likeability can reduce the barrier to an initial interaction. Familiarity can make participation easier. A good experience can give people something worth remembering or talking about.

But none of these should substitute for competence, usefulness, reliability, or credibility.

The event can open the relationship. The organization still has to earn what happens after it.

That is also why community activity and direct relationship marketing can connect. In Is direct marketing irrelevant in the age of AI?, I look at what happens after the original service relationship: continued contact, repeat need, and referral.

A prize can give someone a reason to enter the room. The experience can give them a reason to remember it. Credibility still has to take over afterward.

08

How I would apply it elsewhere

Start with what already matters to the community.

Interest: What does this audience already care about?

Commitment: How much are we asking from someone who may barely know us?

Experience: What can they observe about us through the interaction?

Value: What do they receive even if they never convert?

Emotion: Should the experience feel enjoyable, reassuring, empowering, connected, or something else?

Evidence: What does the interaction allow the audience to judge for themselves?

Relationship: What becomes different after the experience?

Advocacy: Is there anything meaningful enough that someone would naturally talk about it later?

This produces a different kind of event brief.

Instead of asking only how many people should attend, the strategic question becomes:

What should people understand, feel, experience, or be able to say about the relationship after they leave?

Attendance can still be a metric.

It just should not be the whole strategy.

The broader lesson

Create tangible experiences of value before asking for trust.

The two anonymized experiences looked very different.

One was built around a familiar social activity. The other was built around useful educational information.

One began with enjoyment. The other began with utility.

But they solved a similar relationship problem.

They created a low-commitment way to experience something about the brand before demanding a higher-commitment decision.

That matters particularly for intangible offerings.

A person cannot always test the professional service before hiring the provider. They cannot fully know another person's future judgment before deciding whether that person deserves trust.

So the surrounding interactions become important.

Events can demonstrate approachability. Educational content can demonstrate usefulness. Repeated presence can create familiarity. Consistent behavior can create evidence.

And evidence accumulated over time can contribute to trust.

Community relationships become stronger when the organization starts with what matters to people—not with what the organization wants to promote.

When the offering is intangible, create tangible experiences of value before asking for trust.

Scope

Where this framework applies—and where more study is needed.

The examples in this Insight come from work involving service-oriented and public-facing brands where the offering, credibility, or future outcome could not be evaluated as easily as a physical consumer product.

That context matters.

The framework should not be treated as evidence that events, familiarity, nostalgia, educational content, or low-commitment interactions will produce the same outcomes across industries.

Ecommerce, transactional retail, subscription products, healthcare, financial services, B2B services, nonprofit organizations, and other contexts can involve different customer journeys, risk levels, regulatory constraints, and definitions of trust.

Further testing is warranted before treating the patterns described here as universal performance conclusions.

Experience note. The real-world examples in this Insight have been deliberately anonymized. Names, organizations, locations, event titles, campaign names, identifying creative, political context, and other details have been generalized while preserving the strategic problem, intervention, and lesson.
Research use note. The research referenced here supports three narrower ideas: the challenge of evaluating intangible services, the role familiarity can play in affective response, and the possibility that a smaller initial action can influence later behavior. The Relationship Entry Ladder and its application to community marketing are my own strategic synthesis. The cited researchers did not test the specific events, campaigns, service businesses, or public-facing professional strategies described here, so I use their work as theoretical lenses rather than as evidence that a particular event format will necessarily produce attendance, trust, advocacy, or conversion.
